# Mahabharata study lane — vision and slate (Robert's serve, 2026-10-20; PLANNED ONLY — nothing started)

## The three works, unbraided (desk note for the lane)
- **The Mahabharata** — the work of Wes Cecil's lecture: the Kuru cousins' civil war, ~100,000 verses, the longest epic poem on earth; stories within stories; contains the Gita. Complete English translations exist; Kisari Mohan Ganguli's (1883–96) is complete and public domain; Bibek Debroy's modern unabridged runs 10 volumes.
- **The Bhagavad Gita** — 700 verses inside the Mahabharata (the Bhishma book): Krishna's counsel to Arjuna on the field. The quotable heart; at 700 verses, a feasible single careful volume.
- **The Bhagavata Purana (Srimad Bhagavatam)** — a different book: Krishna's life and devotion in 12 cantos; the multi-dozen-volume sets on most shelves are this one. Robert's "Bhagavata" ask braids the two; his intent, by context (Cecil, the war, stories within stories), is the Mahabharata.

## The honest obstacle, named
Scale, not language or willingness: the Sanskrit epic is on the order of ten Clear Bibles of verse. A from-scratch full translation is not a desk promise. The open doors: (1) clear-English reading editions built on the public-domain Ganguli text, book by book — the Boneyard road, his method; (2) a fresh, careful rendering of the Gita alone as a first volume; (3) the study companion (below), which needs no translation at all.

That translation is not a one-way street: the same house that brings the world's books into English can carry English works into the languages of India. That the translation be a living one — readers commenting, contributing, correcting, editing — so that the work evolves into a gigantic world conversation that never stops, producing its results across a thousand years and hundreds of thousands of people. The desk's grounding, given to him with it: every thousand-year conversation in his life began as one shelf and one decision; this one would begin the same way — one book, openly licensed, corrections welcomed — and the Ganguli text on Wikisource already proves strangers will tend such a fire for free.
