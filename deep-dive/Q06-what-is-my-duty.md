# Deep-Dive Q6 — What is my duty, and what is the best way to fulfill it?

> **DRAFT — Robert's study. Nothing published.** Built under the Cecil Rule: nothing implied, nothing inferred, everything in evidence; Cecil quoted in short phrases with timestamps; interpretation marked where it occurs. Evidence base: `CECIL-MAHABHARATA-RECORD.md` (the fourteen served movements with margin notes, including the record's fork note on Mandavya's curse). FOR and AGAINST were worked independently at full strength; the CROSS is the coordinator's comparison.

**The question (Cecil's words, 18:40 — the core question, why one asks sages at all):** "What is my duty — and what is the best way to fulfill it?"

---

## FOR

This is the master question of a human life in the epic's frame as Cecil presents it, because the lecture leaves no competing question standing beside it. Cecil defines dharma plainly: if you do what you're supposed to, when you're supposed to, in the way you're supposed to, good things happen — and if you don't, bad things happen (Movement 2; chapter "The concept of Dharma," 8:53). That definition already contains its promise and its verdict. Interpretation: a question that carries both the act and its consequence in one sentence is not one moral inquiry among many; it is the inquiry by which the others are judged.

The question is unavoidable, and therefore central. Cecil states that the universe is ruled by dharma and that "there is no escaping dharma" (Movement 2; Movement 3). Everything is subject to it. If escape is not an option the poem offers, then refusal, delay, and speculation about an outside do not answer a life. The only live question left is fulfillment — first, what the duty is, and second, how best to do it. Interpretation: inescapability is what converts the question at 18:40 from advice into a master question. You do not decide whether to have a dharma; you decide only whether you will know it and meet it well.

It is also knowable, because it is particular. Cecil teaches that dharma is built around who a person is — caste, sex, age, job, and who you are interacting with — so that everyone has a different dharma, being in a different place, doing different things, with different people (Movement 5). That particularity is not an obstacle to knowledge; it is the method of knowledge. A duty is not found by asking what anyone in general should do, but by locating exactly where you stand and with whom you stand there. Even the widest claim in the movement points the same way: "even the god of Dharma is subject to dharma" (Movement 5). The question therefore binds gods and men alike, and no rank exempts its bearer from having to ask it.

The question's second half — "what is the best way to fulfill it?" (18:40) — is where Cecil redefines freedom. Against the Diderot pole with which he opens, freedom as freedom *from* kings and priests (Movement 2), Cecil sets the epic's answer: "you do not free yourself by getting rid of the priests and the kings; you free yourself by getting rid of the things that interfere with doing what you should do" (Movement 5). Those interferences, he lists, come almost all from inside: anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, laziness (Movement 5). Movement 6 restates the contrast as interior bondage against exterior bondage. Interpretation: on this account the second half of the question is not a technique added to duty; it is freedom itself made practical. To ask how best to fulfill your duty is to ask what in you must be cleared so that you can do it.

The tradition's own practice confirms the centrality. Cecil calls this "the only question you ask the sages and brahmins, the people who know" (Movement 6). That is a consultation economy running on a single question: those who do not yet know their duty go to those who are held to know, and they ask nothing else first. Cecil adds that "the external is irrelevant" in this focus; the tradition is "truly focused on your internal being" (Movement 6). Interpretation: a culture does not reserve its sages for a minor question. That this question is the one brought to them is evidence, inside the lecture's own presentation, of its rank.

Krishna's answer to Arjuna is the model fulfillment (Movement 7; chapter "Duty and the Bhagavad Gita," 19:13). Arjuna lowers his bow and refuses to kill his kin (Movement 6). Krishna answers that Arjuna is a warrior, in a just war, and that it is his duty to fight — duty rightly understood, even in that most extreme case (Movement 7). Cecil's verdict is unsparing: Arjuna's refusal, to his credit in feeling, "turned out to be the wrong answer" (Movement 7). Krishna also corrects the metaphysics that made the duty seem impossible — the soul is immortal, the physical incarnation is not the self (Movement 7; Gita 2 as Cecil summarizes it). Interpretation: the Gita scene does not discard the question; it answers both halves at once — it names the duty, and it removes the mistaken understanding that blocked its fulfillment.

Even dissent lives inside the question. Mandavya, impaled though innocent, does not abandon duty-accounting; he demands one (Movements 3–4; story-voiced questions, 12:44–14:32). He asks what he did, is told of a childhood cruelty, and rejects the proportion outright (Movement 4). Interpretation: that is still the master question at work, turned upward — what was my deed, and does this fulfillment of dharma match it? The sage's protest does not step outside the frame. It argues from within it, which is the strongest backhanded proof the lecture offers that the poem's characters all live inside this question.

**FOR — strongest points**

- **Inescapability makes it master:** there is "no escaping dharma" (Movements 2–3), so fulfillment, not escape, is the only live question (18:40).
- **Particularity makes it knowable:** duty is built from caste, sex, age, job, and interaction, and differs because each person's place differs (Movement 5).
- **Its second half redefines freedom:** freedom comes from removing internal interferences — anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, laziness — not from removing kings and priests (Movements 5–6).
- **The sages confirm its rank:** it is "the only question you ask the sages and brahmins, the people who know" (Movement 6).
- **The Gita proves its stakes:** Arjuna's sympathetic refusal "turned out to be the wrong answer," and Krishna answers by naming the duty and correcting the metaphysics of the soul (Movement 7; Gita 2; 19:13).

## AGAINST

The strongest form of this question deserves to be stated before it is opposed. In Cecil's telling, it is not a request for a rulebook for one occasion; it is the central discipline of a life. Dharma, in his plain definition, means that "if you do what you're supposed to, when you're supposed to, in the way you're supposed to, good things happen — and if you don't, bad things happen" (movement 2, the Vyasa and dharma excerpt). The question at 18:40 therefore assumes three things at once: that a duty exists, that it can be known, and that fulfilling it is the road to a good outcome. The record's own stories put pressure on all three assumptions — and that, in interpretation, is why the question should not be enthroned.

First, the question begins in deference. Cecil's form of it is precise: it is "the only question you ask the sages and brahmins, the people who know" (movement 6, the interior-bondage excerpt). The asker does not begin by judging; he begins by locating the class authorized to judge for him. Set that beside the lecture's own opening pole. Cecil opens with Diderot's line — "Man will never be free until the last king is strangled with the entrails of the last priest" — as the Enlightenment creed of freedom *from* kings and priests, and the record notes the line's documentary root in the *Testament* of Jean Meslier, himself a priest (movement 2). Interpretation: a question whose first move is to hand the verdict upward may train obedience more reliably than judgment. The poem's central scene shows the cost of that training. Arjuna looks at his kin across the field and reaches his own conclusion — he will not fight, and he lowers his bow (movement 6). Cecil records the poem's verdict on that conclusion without softening it: Arjuna, "to his credit, says no — and that turned out to be the wrong answer" (movement 7, Krishna's answer). The asker's judgment was not refined by the question. It was overruled by it.

Second, the answer is largely assigned before the asking begins. Cecil is explicit that dharma is particular: duty and obligation are "built around who you are — caste, sex, age, job, who you are interacting with" (movement 5, the counselor excerpt). If the decisive facts are birth, sex, and station, then the question arrives after its answer has been substantially written by the social order into which the asker was born. Cecil himself voices the modern objection the poem refuses: "who imposed this caste system anyway?" (movement 7). Karna is the human price of that refusal. Cecil lets the thread slip in a parenthesis — the Pandavas are "five of them; well, there's kind of six" (movement 6) — and the record names the sixth: Karna, Kunti's hidden firstborn, fighting on the other side, killed by brothers who did not know he was their brother (movement 7, desk check). Interpretation: a man whose true station was concealed from him lived inside a duty written by a false account of his birth. The question gave him no procedure for discovering that the premise of his duty was wrong.

Third, fulfillment itself is indicted by its result. In movement 7 Cecil gives the count, hedged, and the record's desk check corrects it: of the hundred sons of Gandhari, none survive; almost the entire Pandava army is destroyed; and the brothers kill Karna unknowing. This is not a case of duty misunderstood or half-done. Arjuna is corrected, accepts the correction, fights — and the completed answer leaves the field in ruin. Interpretation: if the question answered perfectly produces catastrophe, the failure cannot be quarantined in the answer. It reaches back into the question, which never asks whether the duty itself should stand.

The poem's smaller stories make the same point from two directions. Mandavya, in Cecil's telling, had "done everything right — meditating, pure" — and was impaled for a theft he did not commit (movements 3–4, story-voiced questions at 12:44–14:32). When he demands an accounting, the ledger's answer is a childhood cruelty to insects, and he rejects the proportion outright, telling the god, in Cecil's register, "you have lost your way, god of dharma" (movement 4). The correction does not enter the system because somebody asked the duty question well. It enters as a victim's curse — and even there, the record's fork note shows the lecture fusing the curse's landing, Vidura in the epic, into Yudhishthira (movement 4, desk fork note). Meanwhile Vidura, the counselor of movement 5, asked and answered correctly for decades: he warned against the dice game and the war, and "they never listen to him, over and over." Correct knowledge of duty, faithfully delivered, had no traction on what happened.

Finally, Cecil warns his listeners at the outset that "anything you say about the Mahabharata is wrong, because some other part of it contradicts it" — a poem that "includes everything" (movement 1, the background movement). Interpretation: a question whose answer changes depending on which part of an encyclopedia-sized, self-contradicting poem you consult cannot bear the weight of a throne. It can be a serious question. The record does not support making it the only one.

**AGAINST — strongest points**

- **The question installs an oracle.** It is asked of "the sages and brahmins, the people who know" (18:40; movement 6), against the lecture's own Diderot pole of freedom from kings and priests (movement 2).
- **The answer is pre-assigned by birth and station.** Duty is built around "caste, sex, age, job" before any asking happens (movement 5); Karna lived and died inside a duty written by a concealed birth (movements 6–7).
- **The asker's own judgment is the designated loser.** Arjuna said no to killing his kin, "and that turned out to be the wrong answer" (movements 6–7).
- **Perfect fulfillment ends in catastrophe.** All hundred sons dead, both armies near-annihilated, a brother killed unknowing (movement 7, desk check on Cecil's count).
- **The system corrects itself only by curse, not by the question.** Mandavya fulfilled his duty, was impaled, and amended dharma by cursing its god (movements 3–4; 12:44–14:32); Vidura answered rightly and was ignored "over and over" (movement 5).

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **Where FOR is strongest: centrality, honestly documented.** The lecture really is organized around this question — it is "the only question you ask the sages" (Movement 6), its second half really does carry Cecil's redefinition of freedom (Movements 5–6), and dharma really is presented as particular and therefore knowable (Movement 5). AGAINST concedes all of this and attacks the throne, not the question's existence. On the descriptive claim — this is the epic's master question as Cecil teaches it — **FOR stands.**
- **Where AGAINST is strongest: the three assumptions.** The question presupposes a duty that exists, can be known, and leads to good outcomes when fulfilled (Movement 2's definition). AGAINST produces a record exhibit against each: existence-by-assignment (caste fixes the content before asking, Movement 5); knowability (Karna's premises were concealed facts, Movements 6–7); outcomes (fulfillment completed at Kurukshetra is the catastrophe, Movement 7 desk check; fulfillment perfected in Vidura changes nothing, Movement 5). No FOR passage in the record answers all three.
- **Head-to-head — inescapability:** FOR: since there is "no escaping dharma" (Movements 2–3), fulfillment is the only live question. AGAINST: inescapability is exactly what should provoke the prior question — who built the inescapable frame — which is Diderot's question, and the lecture itself opens with it (Movement 2). **Edge: a standoff of first premises; the record supplies both poles and adjudicates neither.**
- **Head-to-head — Mandavya's curse, the group's most contested exhibit:** FOR reads the sage's protest as duty-accounting conducted *inside* the frame — he demands the ledger, argues proportion, stays in the question's grammar (Movements 3–4). AGAINST reads the same scene as the system's correction arriving only as a victim's *curse* — from outside obedience — and notes the record's fork: Cecil fuses the curse's epic landing (Vidura) into Yudhishthira to keep the telling tidy (Movement 4, desk fork note). The factual core supports both readings; the difference is whether a curse laid on a god counts as the frame working or the frame failing. **Edge: genuinely divided — flagged here rather than settled, as the Cecil Rule requires.**
- **Head-to-head — outcomes as judges:** Dharma's own definition (Movement 2) ties duty to consequences: do it, and good things happen. AGAINST turns that definition on the question — if outcomes judge, then Kurukshetra's field and Vidura's ignored decades judge harshly. FOR's strongest available reply is that the definition's "good things" are measured across incarnations and the long wheel (Movement 6's rising through lives), a scale on which no single war is the verdict — but the record's excerpts state that scale only in passing, and neither worker could build the reply at full strength from the evidence served. **Edge: AGAINST on the record as it stands; the point most likely to move if more of the lecture is served.**
- **Head-to-head — the oracle:** FOR treats consultation as the question's social proof (Movement 6). AGAINST treats the same fact as the question's original sin: the verdict is handed upward to "the people who know" before the asker judges (Movement 6; Movement 2's Meslier note — even the anti-priest line was a priest's). **Edge: AGAINST on the politics, FOR on the anthropology — both are reading the same sentence.**
- **What remains open:** (1) whether the question needs a companion — *who assigned this duty, and may it be appealed?* — and whether the poem itself supplies that companion in Mandavya's curse, making the true master-form a pair of questions rather than one; (2) whether deference to accredited knowers can be squared with the lecture's own Enlightenment pole, or whether Cecil's contrast is meant to stay unresolved; (3) whether outcomes, on the poem's own long scale, vindicate fulfillment in the cases (Vidura, Karna) where this life shows only loss; (4) whether the throne is the problem — the question demoted from *only* to *first* would survive nearly all of AGAINST's case, and the record nowhere shows Cecil considering the demotion.
