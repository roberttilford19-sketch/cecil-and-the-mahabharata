# Q10 — Who do you defend, and trust, and support?

> **DRAFT — Robert's study material, Deep-Dive Group D. Nothing published. Cecil's words are his; quotes are short phrases with timestamps, per the Cecil Rule: nothing implied, nothing inferred, everything in evidence; interpretation marked where it occurs.**

**The question (Wes Cecil, "The Ancients: Vyasa and The Mahabharata"):** "Who do you defend, and trust, and support — and who defends, trusts, and supports you?" (42:18–42:27; the "Loyalty and kinship" chapter opens at 39:10; the theme it serves, "wealth is people," opens at 24:48.)

---

## FOR — the circuit is the wealth

### (a) The question's own shape

Cecil poses the question at 42:18–42:27, inside the "Loyalty and kinship" chapter that begins at 39:10, in two directions at once: "Who do you defend, and trust, and support — and who defends, trusts, and supports you?" (42:18–42:27). The pairing is the point. A one-directional question would only audit loyalty given — what a person owes or sacrifices for others. Cecil's second clause audits loyalty received, in the same sentence. Interpretation, marked as such: the form refuses to treat defense, trust, and support as a virtue one person performs alone; it treats them as a relationship that either runs both ways or fails as a whole. The episodes below bear that out, under the lecture's central claim, introduced in the chapter beginning at 24:48 — "wealth is people" (24:48).

### (b) The case, movement by movement

Movement 9 demonstrates the thesis by subtraction. The five Pandava brothers and Draupadi endure twelve years of exile, and the exile itself is not what breaks them: together, with their brothers and with Draupadi, it is bearable (movement 9). Arjuna's departure for the Himalayas is itself an act of defense — he leaves to meditate and win weapons because they are going to have to fight, and the company will need them (movement 9). Yet that act removes him, and the others fall into grief: now that Arjuna is gone, the joy has gone out of their lives (movement 9). The lesson is reciprocal in both directions. The defender's absence is felt as loss precisely because his presence was support; their grief proves support is not an extra added to a bearable life — it is what made the unbearable bearable.

Movement 10 carries the proof to its heaviest form, in two refusals. In the record's margin notes, Yudhishthira first refuses, at heaven's very gate, to abandon the stray dog who has followed him — defending a companion who can offer him nothing in return (movement 10). Then, shown his brothers suffering in hell, he refuses heaven itself: he would rather suffer in hell with those he loves than be in heaven on his own, because there is no paradise if he is not with his brothers (movement 10). Reciprocity is completed from the brothers' side: in the text's vision-test, their voices beg him to stay because his presence eases them (movement 10, desk margin notes). He defends them by staying; his staying is itself the support they receive — the same act, seen from both ends.

Movements 12, 13, and 14 prove the thesis from the negative side, where reciprocity breaks. Movement 12 states the mechanism as a chain: wealth threatens judgment; when judgment fails, a person insults the people around him, treats them poorly, or forgets them; then they leave; and then all his power goes away (movement 12). Power is downstream of company: the people depart, and the power follows them.

Movement 13 shows that chain in heaven itself. Brihaspati, Indra's guru, supports and guides the king of the gods — the record describes him as the one who cared for Indra, guided him, and helped him with wisdom (movement 13). At the height of his power Indra fails in the smallest reciprocal act: he does not rise to show his guru respect (movement 13). Brihaspati reads the slight and simply leaves. The withdrawal alone unmakes the king: Indra's power begins to fade, and those around him realize he does not know what to do (movement 13). No enemy defeats him. The supporter withdraws, and the power drains after the wisdom.

Movement 14 completes the sequence. Vishvarupa, the replacement guru, supports the gods, advises them, and all is good again — showing the restoration came from restored support, not from Indra himself (movement 14). But Indra never quite trusts him, suspects the half-demon guru of secretly siding with the demons, and kills him (movement 14). The supported man destroys his own supporter. Cecil's catalogue for the act, in movement 14, is arrogance, fear, and envy; on the record's margin notes the killing, the slaying of a priest, brings the worse enemy Vritra in its train (movement 14, desk margin notes). Movement 11 states the positive form: being with those who care about you, who give you wisdom and grant you insight, is true wealth, the foundation of the quality of your life (movement 11).

### (c) The answer at full strength

At full strength, the lecture and the epic answer with an inventory and a warning. Name the people you defend, trust, and support, and name the people who defend, trust, and support you — because on the evidence of movements 9 through 14, those are not two separate accounts; they are one account, and it is your wealth and your power. While it holds, exile is bearable together, a defender's errand is undertaken for the company, and a man can refuse heaven rather than break the company that makes heaven heaven (movements 9 and 10). When it breaks — by a slight unrepaired, a supporter forgotten, insulted, distrusted, or killed — the grief, the fading, and the fall follow in that order (movements 12, 13, and 14). You do not know what you have, or how long you will keep the power to act, until you can answer both halves.

## AGAINST — the circuit does not close

### (a) The counter-thesis, stated fairly

The lecture answer to Cecil's question at 42:18–42:27 — "Who do you defend, and trust, and support — and who defends, trusts, and supports you?" — deserves its strongest form: defense, trust, and support are not private virtues but relations. They run both ways, bind a company together, and in the chapters headed "People as true wealth" (24:48) and "Loyalty and kinship" (39:10), they constitute what a person has.

The counter-thesis is not that people do not matter. The epic, on the lecture's own evidence, proves they do. It is that reciprocity is neither necessary, nor sufficient, nor safe as the poem's measure of that mattering. Inside the lecture itself, the most righteous defense is unreturned, the highest defense is offered to one who can return nothing, a perfect reciprocal bond binds a man to catastrophe, a faithful supporter is killed by the man he supports, and those most loved still fall on the road. The circuit described by the question is, at best, a partial description — not the poem's law.

### (b) The internal counter-testimony, strongest first

First, Vidura (movement 5). The record identifies him as the counselor everyone asks: he warns against gambling against the Kaurava brothers, counsels Dhritarashtra against the dice game and against Duryodhana's whole course, and strives to prevent the war. The lecture's summary is repeated failure: they never listen to him, over and over. He is, on the desk fork note, Dharma himself reborn low by Mandavya's curse — dharma subject to dharma. Interpretation, marked as such: if support were wealth because it returns, Vidura should be the richest man in Hastinapura. The poem instead makes him its clearest creditor never paid. Defense at full strength meets a broken circuit, and the break is not his fault.

Second, the dog (movement 10, desk margin). On the final road, a stray dog follows Yudhishthira. At heaven's gate, Indra's price is that Yudhishthira abandon the dog to enter, and he refuses. The dog, the margin notes, is Dharma in disguise, and the poem tests the same refusal twice — the dog first, then the brothers in the vision of hell. The dog as dog can reciprocate nothing: no counsel, no army, no defense in return. Interpretation: the summit of the poem is therefore not a closed exchange but one-way fidelity to a dependent who has nothing to give back. That cuts against the paired symmetry of the question's wording.

Third, Karna (movements 6–7, the hidden-sixth thread). Cecil lays the thread — five Pandavas, with a kind of sixth — and movement 7 draws it tight: Karna is Kunti's hidden firstborn, fighting on the other side, killed unknowing by his own brothers. Karna defends Duryodhana because Duryodhana defended him when no one else would. Here reciprocity works exactly as advertised: a defense received, a defense returned, unto death. And it binds him to the losing, unjust side. Interpretation: the circuit is morally blind. It can certify gratitude while failing to certify justice; a returned debt is not, by itself, a right cause.

Fourth, Indra and Vishvarupa (movement 14). After Brihaspati leaves Indra's court, the gods take Vishvarupa as guru. He advises, and, in the served telling, everything returns to good. Indra never quite trusts him, suspects him of siding with the demons, and kills him. The record states the hook fairly: in the epic telling Vishvarupa did secretly direct a share of offerings to his mother's kin, the asuras. It also states the price: the killing is brahmahatya, the gravest sin in the poem's ledger, and Tvashta engenders Vritra to avenge his son. The supporter gains no protection from having supported. In the poem's own heaven, a defender destroyed by the defended unleashes a worse enemy than the one feared.

Fifth, the road (movement 10, desk margin). Yudhishthira loves and will not abandon his brothers and Draupadi, yet each falls on the road anyway, each with an assigned cause: Draupadi for partiality toward Arjuna, Sahadeva for pride in his wisdom, Nakula for pride in his beauty, Arjuna for the unkept boast, Bhima for gluttony and pride. His later refusal — to suffer in hell with those he loves rather than be in heaven alone — is noble, and the margin notes it dissolves a vision-test. It does not undo the falls. Defense, however faithful, does not guarantee arrival; support does not save.

### (c) The modern objection, briefly

A reciprocal frame can shrink, in modern hands, into accounting: support tallied, loyalty invoiced, debts called in when convenient. The poem's dog answers that shrinkage from inside. And a circuit-ethic leaves unpriced precisely those who most need defending and have no company, status, or strength with which to repay. That is an objection to the frame, not to the people inside it.

### (d) What the counter leaves standing

This counter leaves standing the lecture's demonstrations by subtraction: the forest company's grief when Arjuna leaves (movement 9), and Yudhishthira's discovery that heaven alone is no heaven (movement 10). People as wealth survives. What does not survive untouched is reciprocity as the measure of that wealth. On the poem's own testimony, the highest defenses are sometimes unreturned, sometimes unreturnable, sometimes binding to ruin, and sometimes fatal to the defender — and they are commanded, and honored, anyway.

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **Where FOR is strongest — collapse always runs through a broken relation.** Every fall in the evidence is a support-relation failing: judgment bent, a supporter slighted and gone (Brihaspati, movement 13), people leaving and power following (movement 12), the company's joy going out with one man's absence (movement 9). As an account of how wealth and power are actually lost, FOR has no unaddressed exhibit: AGAINST offers no fall that happens with the circuit intact and honored.
- **Where AGAINST is strongest — reciprocity as the measure.** The poem's most honored defenses are precisely the ones the circuit cannot price: Vidura, never listened to, over and over (movement 5), and the dog, who can return nothing (movement 10). AGAINST's Vidura is FOR's open wound — FOR's case never touches him, and he is the record's purest defender.
- **The collision exhibit is movement 10, and both sides hold a piece of it.** FOR reads the brothers' voices — his presence eases them — as reciprocity completed at the level of presence. AGAINST reads the dog at the gate as reciprocity refuted at the summit. Both readings are in evidence; neither cancels the other, because the test is doubled in the text itself: the same refusal, twice, at increasing weight — once where return is impossible (the dog), once where return is presence itself (the brothers).
- **FOR's own evidence concedes AGAINST's dog.** FOR's section folds the dog in as "defending a companion who can offer him nothing in return" — stated inside the FOR case, it quietly gives up the paired symmetry the question's wording promises. FOR is strongest when it argues from loss; it is weakest when it claims the account balances.
- **Karna is the circuit working perfectly — and that is the problem.** Both sides agree on the facts (movements 6–7): a defense received, a defense returned, unto death. FOR does not claim him; AGAINST's point stands unanswered in the record: reciprocity certifies gratitude, not justice. A returned debt is not a right cause.
- **Vishvarupa cuts both ways, at different depths.** FOR uses him as the chain's last link — distrust the supporter and the fall completes (movement 14). AGAINST uses the same corpse to show the defender is not protected by defending. The record supports both: the killing is at once Indra's self-ruin and Vishvarupa's unmerited end, and the poem prices it as the gravest sin in its ledger.
- **What the question may be doing.** Read as Cecil poses it — an audit, not a promise — the two sides converge more than they clash: FOR proves you cannot know your wealth without both halves of the inventory; AGAINST proves the second half is not a guarantee but an examination. Whether Cecil intends a description of the world or a test of the man remains open in the lecture as served.
- **What remains open.** (1) Whether reciprocity is the poem's law or only its usual weather — the honored exceptions (Vidura, the dog) are not marginal figures. (2) Whether "presence" (movement 10) counts as return, or redefines return so widely that AGAINST's unreturned cases dissolve into it. (3) Whether the circuit needs a justice-test outside itself — Karna's case says gratitude alone cannot tell a man which side to defend.
