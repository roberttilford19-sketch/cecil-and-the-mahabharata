# Q14 — Vritra, mid-war: why are we fighting?

> **DRAFT — Robert's study, Deep-Dive Group E ("the story asking back"), 2026-10-21. Nothing published. Drafted under the Cecil Rule: nothing implied, nothing inferred, everything in evidence; interpretation marked where it occurs.**

**The question** (essay sheet Q14; Wes Cecil, "The Ancients: Vyasa and The Mahabharata," 33:34): Vritra — engendered by the grieving Tvashta to avenge his murdered son — asks, mid-war: why are we fighting?

**Evidence base:** CECIL-MAHABHARATA-RECORD.md, served excerpts 12 (wealth misleads; the Indra–Vishnu succession), 13 (Indra and the guru who simply left — Brihaspati), 14 (the invisible guru; Vishvarupa; the killing), and the question census at 33:34. The record states only that the fight ends by *tricking* Vritra; loophole details from the wider tradition are not record evidence and are not used below.

---

## FOR — the poem answers Vritra

At 33:34 in the lecture census, Vritra asks, mid-war, "why are we fighting," and the census notes the fighting is stopped by tricking him. The FOR case is that the poem, as Cecil presents it in the record, does not leave that question hanging. It has already answered it, in sequence, before Vritra appears.

Interpretation: the answer is given in two forms — by genealogy, who Vritra is and why he exists, and by price, what Indra's acts cost and what it takes to end the fight he created.

The chain begins in served excerpt 12, on wealth misleading. Cecil lays out the mechanism: wealth and power threaten judgment; threatened judgment insults or forgets the people who should be valued; those people leave; power then goes with them. The record's margin note states the order plainly: power is downstream of company.

Served excerpt 13 applies that chain to Indra himself. Indra is at his height, king of the gods. His guru Brihaspati, preceptor of the gods, enters, and Indra does not rise to show respect. The guru reads the slight, leaves without argument or fight, and makes himself unavailable. Indra's power begins to fade. No enemy defeats him in this movement. His guide is no longer in the room, and the power drains after the wisdom.

Served excerpt 14 continues the sequence. With the demons attacking and the gods faring poorly, a replacement guru is obtained: Vishvarupa, called Trisiras, son of Tvashta, of asura descent on his mother's side. The record's margin note, citing the Udyoga telling, states fairly that Vishvarupa did secretly direct a share of the offerings to his mother's kin, the asuras. Indra suspects him, never quite trusts him, and kills him. Cecil's catalogue for the act, as recorded in excerpt 14, is arrogance, fear, and envy — the interior interferences named earlier in the lecture, now in a king.

The record names the killing for what the poem calls it: brahmahatya, the slaying of a priest, the gravest of sins in the poem's own ledger. Its price arrives by name in the same margin note: Tvashta, in grief and rage, engenders Vritra to avenge his son, and Indra's real fall begins.

Read so: when Vritra then asks at 33:34, "why are we fighting," the poem has supplied the cause in full. You are fighting because a king at his height would not rise for his guru, lost him, took a replacement he did not trust, and killed that priest. Vritra is not an unexplained monster intruding on order. In the genealogy the record lays out, he is the engendered consequence of that killing — the son made to answer a son's death.

The second answer is the price of ending it. The census at 33:34 records that the fighting is stopped by tricking him. The record does not present that ending as clean glory. It places the trick at the end of a ledger that already includes a guru lost by insult, a priest killed, and a sin named as the gravest. Interpretation: a war that begins in a failure of respect and a priest-killing, and can only be closed by trickery against the avenger it created, vindicates the questioner by position. Vritra stands in the poem as the wronged party's son and the living receipt for Indra's sin, asking the one question the preceding episodes have already answered.

**Three strongest evidence anchors:**

- **Served excerpt 13 — Indra and Brihaspati:** Indra at his height does not rise for his guru; the guru simply leaves; Indra's power begins to fade.
- **Served excerpt 14 — Vishvarupa killed, Vritra engendered:** Indra kills the replacement guru Vishvarupa (Trisiras); the record names it brahmahatya, the gravest sin, and states Tvashta engendered Vritra to avenge his son.
- **Question census, 33:34 — Vritra:** "why are we fighting"; the census records the fighting was stopped by tricking him, not by a clean defeat.

## AGAINST — the question stands open

At 33:34, in the question census, Vritra asks: "why are we fighting." The census records the question. It records no answer given to him in dialogue. That silence is the whole case, and the record around it makes the silence worse, not better.

The fighting, on the record, is not ended by an answer. It is ended by tricking him. A trick is not a reply. A trick does not say the war is just, or necessary, or required by any duty the questioner could examine. It bypasses the question and removes the man who asked it. If the poem had an answer it was willing to state to Vritra's face, the record would not need to close the episode this way.

What the record does supply, in served excerpts 12, 13, and 14, is a chain of causes — and a chain of causes is not a justification. Excerpt 13: Indra, at his height, does not rise for his guru Brihaspati. The sage reads the insult exactly, leaves without argument, and Indra's power begins to fade. Excerpt 14: the gods take a replacement guru, Vishvarupa, half demon, who advises well and restores order. Indra never trusts him, suspects him of siding with the demons, and kills him. The desk note on that excerpt is plain: Tvashta, in grief and rage, engenders Vritra to avenge his son. Vritra, then, is not an uncaused evil who interrupts a just order. He is the price of a priest-killing, produced by the king of the gods' own suspicion acted on in fear.

Read so: when Vritra asks why they are fighting, the honest answer available inside the record is humiliating to Indra — because you insulted your teacher, lost him, distrusted his replacement, and killed that replacement. That is a receipt. It shows how the debt was incurred. It does not show why the man holding the receipt owes his life for it, and no one in the record states it to him as an answer. Explanation has been substituted for justification, and the substitution is left standing.

The framework that is supposed to govern this world does not close the gap. Cecil's lecture, in the dharma movement, insists there is no escape from dharma, that even the god of Dharma is subject to dharma. In the Indra chain that framework restrains Indra at no point the record shows. It does not restrain him from the insult in excerpt 13. It does not restrain him from the killing in excerpt 14, which the record names the gravest kind of sin, the slaying of a priest. It does not restrain him, finally, from ending the fight by a trick rather than by speech. Cecil's own catalogue for the killing is arrogance, fear, and envy — the interior interferences the lecture elsewhere lists as what a man must throw off to do his duty. Indra throws off nothing. He acts from all three, and the war follows.

The contrast inside the poem is unsparing. Arjuna lowers his bow on the field and will not kill his kin, and he receives an answer: arguments, instruction, and a revelation, at length. Vritra asks the war's only sane question from the other side and receives no discourse at all. Interpretation: the difference is not that one question is better than the other. It is who is permitted to ask it and live. Being right buys Vritra nothing. He is killed anyway.

That is why the question stands open. The poem preserves it at 33:34, surrounds it with a genealogy that indicts the man who made the war necessary, and then closes the fight not with a reason Vritra could accept or refuse, but with a trick and a death. An unanswered question put by the man who was created to avenge a murder, and then silenced without reply, is not an answered question. It is an exposed one.

**Strongest evidence anchors:**

- **33:34, question census** — Vritra: "why are we fighting"; the census records the question and, on the record, the fighting ended by tricking him, not by an answer in dialogue.
- **Excerpt 14** — Indra kills Vishvarupa, his replacement guru, from untrust and suspicion; Tvashta engenders Vritra to avenge his son, so Vritra is the price of Indra's act, not its justification.
- **Excerpts 12–13** — the chain begins with wealth misleading judgment and Indra failing to rise for Brihaspati, who simply leaves; power fades after the insult, before any enemy acts.

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **FOR is strongest on the poem's structure.** Three served movements (12, 13, 14) lay out a complete cause-chain before the question is ever asked: wealth bends judgment → the seated insult → the guru gone → the distrusted replacement killed → the avenger engendered. On the record, no major question in this group arrives with its antecedents so fully documented.
- **FOR's strongest single fact:** Vritra's very existence in the record is an answer-shaped fact — Tvashta engendered him *to avenge his son*, and the record names the killing he avenges brahmahatya, the gravest sin in the poem's own ledger (excerpt 14 margin). The poem grades Indra's act in its own terms; the questioner stands vindicated by position before he speaks.
- **AGAINST is strongest on the scene itself.** The census at 33:34 records a question and a trick — no reply in dialogue, to anyone, anywhere in the record. AGAINST does not need to dispute the genealogy; it only has to point at the empty space where an answer to the asker would go.
- **AGAINST's strongest single fact:** the contrast the record itself supplies. Arjuna's refusal on the field earns arguments, instruction, and a revelation at length (excerpts 6–8); Vritra's question earns trickery and death. The record documents both treatments and offers no principle that distinguishes them except whose side the asker stands on.
- **Where the two actually divide:** FOR's answer exists for the *reader* — enacted in genealogy and price, visible from outside the war. AGAINST's missing answer is the one owed to the *asker* — a reason stated to Vritra that he could accept or refuse. Interpretation: this is Q13 inverted. Mandavya receives a spoken answer and rejects it; Vritra receives no spoken answer and is structurally vindicated. Neither man gets both.
- **What remains open:**
  - Whether a poem "answers" a question when the answer is enacted for the audience but withheld from the asker. Both workers assume opposite measures; the record exhibits the pattern (here, and at Q13) without naming it.
  - Indra's own price. The record closes the fight with the trick and does not state, in the served excerpts, what the brahmahatya costs Indra afterward — his real fall "begins" (excerpt 14 margin) but its working-out lies outside this evidence base. Printed as open.
  - The suspicion's hook (excerpt 14 margin: Vishvarupa did send offering-shares to the asuras) — FOR does not need it and AGAINST concedes it, and it changes neither case: a true suspicion, acted on in arrogance, fear, and envy, still buys the worse enemy. Both sides leave its moral weight unassigned, and so does the record.
