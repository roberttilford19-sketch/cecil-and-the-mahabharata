# Deep-Dive Q4 — What are we supposed to be pursuing?

> **DRAFT — Robert's study. Nothing published.** Built under the Cecil Rule: nothing implied, nothing inferred, everything in evidence; Cecil quoted in short phrases with timestamps; interpretation marked where it occurs. Evidence base: `CECIL-MAHABHARATA-RECORD.md` (the fourteen served movements with margin notes). FOR and AGAINST were worked independently at full strength; the CROSS is the coordinator's comparison.

**The question (Cecil's words, 59:53, the closing collected questions):** "What are we supposed to be pursuing?"

---

## FOR

On the evidence of Cecil's lecture, the answer is not money, and it is not freedom understood as doing whatever we want. What we are supposed to be pursuing is duty rightly understood, and people as our true wealth — in that order, and against the modern inversion that pursues money first and expects people to follow.

The foundation is dharma. In the movement where Cecil defines it (Movement 2; chapter "The concept of Dharma," 8:53), the universe is ruled by dharma, and there is no escaping it. His plain definition is: "if you do what you're supposed to, when you're supposed to, in the way you're supposed to, good things happen" — and if you don't, bad things happen. Dharma is particular, not general: your duty is built around who you are, where you stand, and who you are with. Interpretation, marked as such: if that definition holds, then the first pursuit in life cannot be acquisition. It must be discovery — finding what is yours to do — because the good outcome follows the duty, not the other way around.

That discovery is also, in this telling, what freedom actually is. In the freedom movement (Movement 5), Cecil sets the Mahabharata against the Enlightenment creed he opens with: freedom here is not removing kings and priests outside us, but removing the interferences inside us — "anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, laziness." You free yourself, he says, by overcoming the baser instincts that impede your duty, and you become good by becoming better at fulfilling that duty unimpeded. The pursuit, then, is interior work in service of an exterior obligation, not escape from obligation altogether.

The lecture makes that pursuit a single, repeatable question. In Movement 6, Cecil says "the only question you ask the sages" is: "what is my duty, and what is the best way to fulfill it?" Not what can I get, not what do I deserve, not how do I get free of this — what is my duty, and how do I fulfill it best. If you do that, good things; if you don't, bad things. The external, he stresses, is irrelevant; the focus is truly on your internal being.

Krishna's answer to Arjuna shows how far that pursuit goes. In the Gita movement (Movement 7; chapter "Duty and the Bhagavad Gita," 19:13), Arjuna lowers his bow rather than kill his kin, and Krishna answers no: you are a warrior, in a just war, and it is your duty to fight — fail in your just duty, rightly understood, and it is bad, "even in that most extreme case." Cecil's distilled contrast (Movement 8) is that our inheritance says throw off external shackles and do what you want, while the epic says find the framework you are supposed to fill and throw off the internal inhibitions so you can fulfill it gloriously: "Duty, duty, duty." Interpretation: the lecture does not soften the cost to make the answer palatable; the extremity of the case is the proof of its weight.

The second pursuit is people. In the wealth movement (Movements 8–9; chapter "People as true wealth," 24:48), Cecil states it flatly: "wealth is people" — the greatness, joy, pleasure, power, and riches of your life are the people in your life. The epic does not merely assert this; it demonstrates it by subtraction. The five Pandava brothers and Draupadi endure twelve years of forest exile together, and exile together is bearable — they have each other. When Arjuna leaves for the Himalayas to win weapons, grief falls: "we don't mind being exiled here in the wilderness when we're all together," but now that Arjuna is gone, the joy has gone out of their lives. It is not exile that is bad, Cecil says; it is not having those we love around us.

The final test carries the same answer to heaven's gate (Movement 10; chapter "Loyalty and kinship," 39:10). Yudhishthira alone reaches paradise, sees his brothers suffering in hell, and refuses: he would rather suffer in hell with those he loves than be in heaven on his own, because there is nothing greater or more noble than being with his brothers. Cecil's verdict: "being in heaven alone is no heaven. There is no paradise. It is only a paradise if you're with your brothers." Interpretation: if even paradise fails the test when it is solitary, then no solitary prize — no fortune, no status, no private heaven — can be the thing we are supposed to be pursuing.

Against this, the lecture names the modern pursuit and calls it an inversion (Movement 11). We think wealth is money, we use money to buy things that make our lives good, and because we know we are not supposed to buy people, "de facto people cannot be wealth." People are expected to fall into place once we get the car — Cecil's image is the Mercedes, with friends shoved in the trunk. The poem warns that the ordering destroys itself (Movement 12). Wealth misleads; it is a threat to judgment; threatened judgment insults, treats poorly, or forgets the people around us; then they leave, and then all your power goes away. Indra proves the chain in heaven itself (Movement 13). At his height he does not rise for his guru Brihaspati, the sage reads the disrespect and simply leaves, and Indra's power begins to fade — no one defeats him; his guide stops being in the room, and the power drains after the wisdom.

So the affirmative answer stands whole: pursue your duty, cleared of the anger, greed, fear, and laziness that impede it, and pursue people — noble, good, and wise company — as the wealth by which a life is measured. Money pursued first corrupts judgment, drives people away, and takes power with them. Duty and people pursued first are, on this lecture's evidence, the only pursuits that survive exile, war, and heaven itself.

**FOR — strongest points**

- **Dharma defines the pursuit:** do what you are supposed to, when and as you are supposed to, and good things follow (Movement 2; "The concept of Dharma," 8:53).
- **The only question for the sages is duty:** "what is my duty, and what is the best way to fulfill it?" — and freedom is clearing the internal interferences, anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, and laziness, that block it (Movements 5–6).
- **Duty holds even at extreme cost:** Krishna requires Arjuna to do his just duty rightly understood even when it means fighting his own kin (Movement 7; "Duty and the Bhagavad Gita," 19:13).
- **Wealth is people, proven by subtraction:** exile together is bearable; Arjuna's absence alone removes the joy (Movements 8–9; "People as true wealth," 24:48), and Yudhishthira refuses heaven without his brothers because paradise alone is no paradise (Movement 10).
- **The money-pursuit defeats itself:** wealth threatens judgment, people are insulted or forgotten and leave, and power goes with them — as Indra loses Brihaspati by not rising, and his power fades (Movements 11–13).

## AGAINST

The lecture's answer is attractive because it sounds humane: pursue dharma, your duty rightly understood, and count people, not money, as your true wealth (Chapters: "The concept of Dharma," 8:53; "Duty and the Bhagavad Gita," 19:13; "People as true wealth," 24:48). The counter-case begins by asking what the record itself will not let us evade: who decides what any of us is "supposed" to do, and by what authority?

Cecil defines dharma, in his plain compression, as doing what you are supposed to, when you are supposed to, in the way you are supposed to (movement: Vyasa, and dharma against Diderot). The record then particularizes that duty: it is built around "caste, sex, age, job, who you are interacting with," so that everyone carries a different dharma because everyone occupies a different assigned place (movement: the counselor nobody hears; dharma particularized). Interpretation: that is not yet an answer to the question of pursuit; it is a deferral of it. The pursuit is given before it is chosen, and the givers are the very authorities the lecture sets up as its opposite pole — kings and priests. Cecil opens with Diderot's strangled-king line as the Enlightenment creed of freedom *from* kings and priests, and the record flags that the line's documentary root is the *Testament* of Jean Meslier, himself a priest (same movement; desk tier note). Even the rebellion, on the record's own showing, was authored from inside the priesthood. The authority problem does not dissolve on either side of Cecil's contrast — but only the dharma side asks us to call submission to the assignment freedom.

The poem, by Cecil's own warning, will not certify any single answer anyway. His standing caution, in the background movement, is that anything you say about the Mahabharata is wrong because some other part contradicts it; the poem "includes everything" and is "the universe." Interpretation: a text that contains everything contains the refutation of its own summary. To extract "pursue dharma" as *the* answer is to do exactly what the lecturer warns cannot honestly be done.

The innocence problem is sharper still, and the poem states it before the Gita ever speaks. Mandavya — identified in the record's margin from the served elements: a great yogi, meditating, pure, in a temple where thieves hid the king's gold — is impaled for a theft he did not commit (story-voiced questions, 12:44–14:32; movement: no escape from dharma; the impaled yogi). Restored, he demands Dharma's accounting and is told the spear repaid a childhood cruelty to insects (movement: the apology, and the curse on Dharma). The sage rejects the proportion outright, tells the god he has lost his way, and curses Dharma to mortal rebirth. Interpretation: here the system is not vindicated by being pursued. It is corrected by being defied — by a man who had done everything right and was crushed anyway, and who amended dharma from outside obedience to it. If innocence offers no protection from the ledger, "do your duty and good things happen" fails on the poem's own first test case.

Nor does doing the duty reliably work even when it is done well and heard by all. Vidura, the counselor of the same movement, is consulted by everyone, warns against the gambling and against the whole Kaurava course, and "they never listen to him, over and over." On the epic's strand, which the record keeps distinct from Cecil's fusion, Vidura *is* Dharma reborn low, born of a servant mother by Mandavya's curse — dharma incarnate, doing dharma's work, unheard (movement: the counselor nobody hears; desk fork note). He did not prevent the war. Pursuit, it turns out, guarantees neither audience nor outcome.

Follow Krishna's answer to Arjuna to its end and the two halves of the lecture's answer collide head-on. Arjuna lowers his bow rather than kill the kin he grew up with; Krishna's central argument is that a warrior in a just war must do his duty and kill them (movement: Krishna's answer — duty, the count, and the soul; chapter 19:13). The record's checked count is not Cecil's hedged ninety-seven of a hundred: Bhima kills **all one hundred** sons of Gandhari; almost the entire Pandava army is destroyed; and the brothers unknowingly kill Karna, their own eldest brother (same movement; desk checks). Cecil himself voices the modern reply at exactly this point: question the duty, throw off the shackles — "who imposed this caste system anyway?" Interpretation: the duty half of the answer orders the destruction of the people whom the wealth half calls life's true riches. The kin on the field are, by the lecture's second teaching, the wealth — and by its first teaching, the target. A pursuit that must liquidate its own treasure to prove its fidelity has not resolved the question at 59:53; it has exposed it.

Karna's thread tightens the authority problem into a single life. Cecil lets it slip as a joke — "five of them; well, there's kind of six" — naming the hidden sixth brother, Kunti's firstborn, fighting on the other side (movement: interior bondage; Arjuna lowers his bow; Karna question voiced at 41:15). If your dharma follows who you are, but who you are has been hidden from you and your place assigned on a false birth, then a lifetime of loyal pursuit can aim a man at his own brothers and end with those brothers killing him unknowing. The record presents Karna not as a villain who refused his duty but as a man destroyed inside a duty assigned on concealed facts. Interpretation: a doctrine of assigned pursuit is only as just as the assignment — and the poem's assignment, in its most famous case, was a lie of omission.

The honest counter-case, then, is not that duty and people do not matter. It is that the poem refuses to let "pursue them" stand as a safe answer: it shows the ledger punishing the innocent, the god corrected by a curse, the best counselor ignored into a war, and duty's perfect performance ending in near-total slaughter of the very people it calls wealth.

**AGAINST — strongest points**

- **The authority deferral:** dharma is defined as doing what you are "supposed to," with the content fixed by caste, sex, age, and station — the question of who assigns it is never answered (movements: dharma against Diderot, 8:53; dharma particularized).
- **The self-contradiction warning:** Cecil states that anything said about the poem is wrong because another part contradicts it; it "includes everything" (background movement).
- **Mandavya's innocence:** a pure, meditating sage impaled for another's theft, charged for childhood cruelty to insects, who remedies the disproportion by cursing Dharma, not by pursuing him (story-voiced questions, 12:44–14:32; movement: the curse on Dharma).
- **The catastrophic count:** duty rightly understood, followed to the end, kills all hundred sons of Gandhari, destroys almost the whole Pandava army, and kills the unknowing brother Karna — prompting Cecil's own voiced modern objection, "who imposed this caste system anyway?" (movement: Krishna's answer, 19:13).
- **The internal collision and the Karna case:** the duty to kill kin destroys the people the same lecture calls true wealth, and Karna's assigned place rested on a hidden birth — pursuit faithfully performed, on false premises, to a fratricidal end (movements: Arjuna lowers his bow; "People as true wealth," 24:48; Karna at 41:15).

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **Where FOR is strongest: the positive content, demonstrated, not asserted.** FOR's wealth half rests on the lecture's two worked proofs — the forest demonstration by subtraction (Movement 9: exile together bearable, Arjuna's absence the whole of the grief) and the final test (Movement 10: heaven refused alone) — and AGAINST nowhere disputes that people are wealth. The money-pursuit, FOR's named enemy, is defended by neither side: AGAINST never answers the Indra chain (Movements 11–13), in which money-ordered pursuit destroys judgment, company, and power in that order. On "not money," the two cases agree.
- **Where AGAINST is strongest: the unnamed assigner.** The question asks what we are *supposed* to pursue; the lecture's "supposed" is filled in by caste, sex, age, and station (Movement 5), and neither Cecil nor FOR names who assigns the content or by what right. AGAINST's Diderot pole (Movement 2) is Cecil's own, not an import — the lecture opens with the counter-voice and never discharges it.
- **Head-to-head — the promise:** FOR's foundation (Movement 2) promises that duty done brings good things. AGAINST's Mandavya (Movements 3–4) is the poem's first test case and the promise fails it: perfect practice, impalement, a ledger entry for a child's insects. FOR's definition survives only read as tendency, not guarantee — and the record does not settle which reading Cecil intends. **Edge: AGAINST.**
- **Head-to-head — the collision:** FOR holds duty and people together as one pursuit (Movements 7–10). AGAINST shows them colliding on the one field where both are tested: duty orders the killing of the kin whom the wealth teaching calls the treasure, and the checked count — all hundred sons dead, Karna killed unknowing (Movement 7, desk checks) — is the collision's size. FOR's reply, that the extremity proves the duty's weight, and AGAINST's reply, that the extremity indicts it, cite the same verses. **Edge: unresolved — this is the question's true center of gravity.**
- **Head-to-head — freedom:** FOR's redefinition (Movement 5: clear the internal interferences) is the lecture's most original move and AGAINST does not dent it as a discipline. AGAINST's counter — that Mandavya had cleared his interior and was crushed by an exterior ledger — limits the redefinition's reach: it governs the agent, not the world that spears him. **Edge: FOR as discipline, AGAINST as theodicy.**
- **The shared counterexample:** Karna (Movements 6–7; 41:15) is carried by AGAINST as the assignment's failure and is never answered by FOR — a duty faithfully pursued on a concealed birth, ending in fratricide. FOR has no movement in the record to set against him.
- **What remains open:** (1) whether "supposed" can be answered at all without naming an assigner, and whether the poem wants one named; (2) whether Mandavya's curse is the system correcting itself from inside (so FOR's frame absorbs it) or the system indicted from outside (so AGAINST's frame does) — Group B itself divides on this exhibit, FOR in Q6 reading it as accounting-within-the-frame, AGAINST here as defiance; (3) whether the self-contradiction warning (Movement 1) caps every answer to 59:53, including this file's; (4) whether the two halves of the lecture's answer are one pursuit stated twice or two pursuits that the poem itself cannot always reconcile.
