# Deep-Dive Q5 — The daily form: what should I do today?

> **DRAFT — Robert's study. Nothing published.** Built under the Cecil Rule: nothing implied, nothing inferred, everything in evidence; Cecil quoted in short phrases with timestamps; interpretation marked where it occurs. Evidence base: `CECIL-MAHABHARATA-RECORD.md` (the fourteen served movements with margin notes). FOR and AGAINST were worked independently at full strength; the CROSS is the coordinator's comparison.

**The question (Cecil's words, 50:42 — the daily form):** Not "what do I want to do today," but: what should I do today? What duties can I fulfill for my friends and my family?

---

## FOR

At 50:42 Cecil gives the question its daily form: not "what do I want to do today," but what should I do today — what duties can I fulfill for my friends and my family? The affirmative case is that the second question is stronger because it starts from dharma, not from appetite. In Movement 2, Cecil defines dharma plainly: "if you do what you're supposed to, when you're supposed to, in the way you're supposed to, good things happen — and if you don't, bad things happen." Interpretation: a day organized by that definition has a standard outside the mood of the morning. Desire asks what the self feels like getting; duty asks what the day, given who one is, actually requires (Movement 2, dharma definition; chapter "The concept of Dharma," 8:53).

As a discipline, the question works because dharma in this lecture is particular, not abstract. In Movement 5, Cecil says duty and obligation are "built around who you are" — caste, sex, age, job, and who you are interacting with — so a different person, in a different place, with different people, has a different dharma. That particularity is what makes the daily form answerable. "What should I do today?" does not demand a universal program; it demands an honest inventory of one's own relations and place, and then the next faithful act inside them (Movement 5, dharma particularized). The tradition's own form, recorded in Movement 6, is exactly that: "what is my duty, and what is the best way to fulfill it?" — the question the tradition asks the sages, with its core form pinned in the census at 18:40 and its daily form at 50:42.

As a diagnostic, the question exposes what the desire-question hides. Movement 5 redefines freedom: you do not free yourself by removing kings and priests outside you; you free yourself "by getting rid of the things that interfere with doing what you should do," and "almost all of those come from inside: anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, laziness." Movement 6 repeats the point at full scale: "The external is irrelevant" — the focus is internal. Interpretation: asked honestly each morning, "what is my duty?" immediately becomes a second, sharper question — what in me is likely to block it today? Arjuna lowering his bow is that blockage wearing armor: what interferes is not an external shackle but grief and attachment inside him (Movement 6, Arjuna lowers his bow; chapter "Duty and the Bhagavad Gita," 19:13). The desire-question cannot perform that diagnosis, because it treats the interfering feelings as the guide instead of the obstacle.

As a way of keeping people, the question is strongest of all, because the epic demonstrates — by subtraction — that people are the good itself. In Movement 9, the five Pandava brothers and Draupadi endure twelve years of exile in the wilderness, and Cecil's telling is precise: "we don't mind being exiled here in the wilderness when we're all together." When Arjuna leaves for the Himalayas to seek weapons, the joy goes out of their lives; "it is not being exiled that is bad; it is not having those we love and care for around us — that's what's bad" (Movement 9, the forest without Arjuna; chapter "People as true wealth," 24:48). Interpretation: if absence is the bad, then presence is not decoration on the day's real work — tending one's friends and family *is* the day's real work, and the duty-question is the form that keeps that work from being postponed.

The final test proves the same point at the largest scale. In Movement 10, Yudhishthira reaches heaven, is shown his brothers suffering in hell, and refuses the exchange: he would rather suffer in hell with those he loves than be in heaven alone, because "there is no paradise" if it is solitary (Movement 10, the final test; chapter "Loyalty and kinship," 39:10). The record's margin adds the smaller, earlier version of the same refusal: at heaven's very gate, Yudhishthira will not abandon the stray dog who followed him in order to enter (Movement 10, desk margin note). Interpretation: the daily question and the final test are one question at two scales. A man who asks each day what duty he can fulfill for his people is rehearsing, in small, the refusal to purchase his own comfort at the price of leaving them.

The Indra chain shows what the rehearsal protects against — in reverse. In Movements 12–13, Indra at his height fails at a duty so small it is almost invisible: his guru enters, and Indra thinks, "I don't need to rise and show him respect — I'm king of the gods." The sage leaves quietly, and only then does Indra's power begin to fade, because he has "ostracized and alienated the one who cared for him, guided him, helped him with wisdom" (Movement 13, Indra and the guru who simply left). Movement 12 draws the chain generally: wealth threatens judgment, failed judgment insults or forgets people, people leave, "and then all your power goes away." Interpretation: neglect does not begin with a dramatic betrayal; it begins with not rising from one's seat. The daily duty-question is the counter-habit — the small, repeated act of rising, before the wisdom has left the room.

Against all of this stands the priced alternative. In Movement 11, Cecil states the modern ordering without softening it: we think wealth is money, we pursue the big expensive car, and we expect friends and family to "sort of fall into place" afterward — "shove them in the trunk; it'll be great." The duty-question reverses that ordering at the only point where reversal is actually possible: the start of the day, before money and acquisition have arranged it. That is the affirmative case in one line — interpretation, plainly marked: desire makes people the expected byproduct of a successful day; duty makes them the day's assigned and honored work, and the epic's forest, its heaven, and its fallen king of the gods all testify that people kept are the only wealth that holds (Movements 9, 10, 11, and 13).

**FOR — strongest points**

- **Dharma gives the day a standard outside appetite** — do what you are supposed to, when and as you are supposed to, and things hold (Movement 2, dharma's plain definition; 8:53).
- **The question is a diagnostic of freedom** — it names and clears the internal interferences, anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, and laziness, that block duty, rather than obeying them as desire (Movements 5–6; core duty question, 18:40).
- **The forest demonstration proves daily company is the good** — twelve years of exile are bearable together; the bad is the absence of those we love, not the circumstance (Movement 9, the forest without Arjuna; 24:48).
- **The final test is the daily question at full scale** — Yudhishthira refuses heaven alone rather than leave his brothers, and, in the record's margin, refuses to abandon a stray dog at the gate (Movement 10, the final test).
- **Indra shows neglect begins small** — a king who will not rise when his guru enters loses wisdom first and power after; the daily act of respect is what keeps both in the room (Movements 12–13, the Indra chain).

## AGAINST

The strongest form of Cecil's question deserves to be met first. At 50:42 he asks, in the record's census, not "what do I want to do today," but what should I do today — what duties can I fulfill for my friends and my family? Set beside the lecture's "wealth is people" movement (chapter heading, 24:48), that is not mere guilt or busywork. It reorients the day away from appetite and toward the people who, in the poem's final test, make heaven itself worth refusing if they are not there. The counter-case is not that responsibility is worthless. It is that the poem Cecil is teaching keeps breaking the question's own premises.

First, the daily form presupposes that the day's duty is knowable in the morning, by asking. Arjuna's crisis shows the opposite case (Duty and the Bhagavad Gita, 19:13). Arjuna rides out already knowing two duties, and they collide: he is a warrior in what Krishna calls a just war, and he is a brother and nephew facing "brothers and my great, wise, noble uncles." He lowers his bow and refuses — no empire, wealth, fame, or glory is worth the killing. Asking what he should do that day did not clarify the day; it produced paralysis on the field. And the resolution the record preserves is not a better morning question. It is Krishna's metaphysics — the soul is immortal, you are not truly killing anybody — followed by Vishnu's self-revelation as the universe, after which Arjuna submits and the battle commences (the revelation movement, following the Gita arguments in the served excerpts). Interpretation: if the poem's central duty-conflict needs a god's cosmology and a theophany to break, then a daily question is the wrong scale of tool for the conflicts that matter most. It works only where duty was never really in doubt.

Second, daily compliance does not protect the compliant. Mandavya did everything right (story-voiced questions, 12:44–14:32). In Cecil's telling he was deep in meditation, pure and revered, when thieves hid the king's stolen gold in his temple; the king's men found it and impaled the innocent sage. Confronted afterward, Dharma's accounting was that as a child Mandavya had killed insects — in Cecil's telling, insects "and a bird" — and the impalement was repayment. The sage rejected the proportion outright and cursed Dharma himself to mortal rebirth so the god could learn better. Interpretation: the fix in this story is not that Mandavya should have asked his duty more carefully the next morning. The fix is a curse laid on the system that kept his ledger. A question that urges daily fulfillment, without an audit of the ledger's fairness, learns nothing from its own exemplary story.

Third, fulfilled duty does not even succeed. Vidura fulfilled the counselor's duty exactly as prescribed (the counselor movement, following the Mandavya excerpts). Everyone asked his advice; he warned them, in the record's paraphrase, don't gamble against the Kaurava brothers, it will cause bad things — "and they never listen to him, over and over." He was right, repeatedly, and the war came anyway. Interpretation: the daily question measures the asker, never the hearers. A man can answer it perfectly every day of his life and still watch everything he warned against happen, because duty done by one person does not bind the people who benefit from ignoring him.

Fourth — and this is the question the daily form hides — who writes the duty-list? Cecil's own opening pole is Diderot (Introduction, 0:00; the concept of Dharma, 8:53): freedom as freedom *from* kings and priests. Yet in Cecil's exposition, the seeker's "only question" is put to "the sages and brahmins, the people who know": "what is my duty, and what is the best way to fulfill it?" And dharma, as Cecil particularizes it, is not self-chosen: your obligations are built around "caste, sex, age, job, who you are interacting with." The poem's own loose thread names the cost. Arjuna is one of five brothers — "kind of six" (Arjuna movement, 19:13) — the sixth being Karna (story-voiced question, 41:15), the hidden eldest whose whole life was shaped by a birth concealed from him, fighting for the wrong side and killed unknowing by his own brothers. Cecil himself voices the modern objection inside the lecture: "who imposed this caste system anyway?" (Krishna's-answer movement, 19:13). Interpretation: a daily question that routes the self through inherited station and accredited knowers is not neutral self-discipline; it is Diderot's problem returned in devotional dress.

Finally, interpretation, stated as such: the question as phrased at 50:42 has no term for the self. Duties "for my friends and my family" can be the very mechanism by which a person is consumed, because the form supplies no place for legitimate wants, rest, or refusal, and no audit for duties imposed by the people who benefit from them. And Cecil's standing warning about the poem itself closes the case (History and structure, 3:17): anything you say about the Mahabharata is wrong, because some other part of it contradicts it. A text that vast will answer tomorrow's "what should I do" oppositely. The daily question borrows a certainty the poem, on its lecturer's own showing, never grants.

**AGAINST — strongest points**

- **Conflicting duties break the question's premise** — Arjuna knows two duties, they collide, he lowers his bow, and only Krishna's soul-doctrine and Vishnu's revelation break the deadlock (Duty and the Bhagavad Gita, 19:13).
- **Perfect compliance did not protect Mandavya** — meditating, pure, innocent, impaled for another's theft, then charged for a child's cruelty to insects; the remedy was a curse on Dharma, not better compliance (Mandavya, 12:44–14:32).
- **Perfect counsel did not prevent the war** — Vidura warned against the gambling "over and over," was right, and was ignored; the question audits the asker, never the hearers (the counselor movement, served excerpts).
- **The duty-list has authors** — the seeker asks "the sages and brahmins, the people who know," duty is assigned by "caste, sex, age, job," and Karna's life was written by a concealed birth; Cecil himself asks, "who imposed this caste system anyway?" (Arjuna/Krishna movements, 19:13; Karna, 41:15; Caste and societal roles, 48:30).
- **The poem contradicts any single answer** — Cecil's standing warning that some other part of the Mahabharata contradicts whatever is said of it denies the daily form the settled certainty it presupposes (History and structure, 3:17).

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **Where FOR is strongest: ordinary days, and the small end of the scale.** The diagnostic use (Movements 5–6: the question immediately exposes today's internal interference) and the Indra counter-habit (Movements 12–13: neglect begins as not rising from a seat) are claims AGAINST never actually engages — its exhibits are all crises and catastrophes. As a discipline for the unremarkable day, the question stands largely unopposed.
- **Where AGAINST is strongest: the question's hidden premise — that the day's duty is knowable by asking.** Arjuna knew his duties and they collided (Movements 6–7); it took a god's metaphysics and a theophany, not a better morning question, to move him. FOR concedes the scale point implicitly: its strongest evidence is the forest and the gate, where duty was never in doubt. **Edge: FOR at daily scale, AGAINST at crisis scale — and the poem is mostly crisis.**
- **Head-to-head — protection:** FOR offers the question as rehearsal for faithfulness (Movement 10 read back into the day). AGAINST answers with Mandavya (Movements 3–4): the best-rehearsed man in the poem was impaled on a false charge and billed for a child's insects. The daily question governs the asker's conduct; it offers no protection against the ledger's errors. **Edge: AGAINST — the diagnostic covers self-blockage only; the spear in the record comes from outside.**
- **Head-to-head — efficacy:** FOR's Indra chain shows the question's habits preserving wisdom and power (Movements 12–13). AGAINST's Vidura is the chain's refutation at the level of outcomes: a lifetime of fulfilled counsel, "ignored over and over," war anyway (Movement 5). The two exhibits divide cleanly: the question reliably forms the *asker*; it does not reliably move the *hearers*. **Edge: FOR on character, AGAINST on outcomes.**
- **Head-to-head — the self:** FOR's form has no place for the asker's wants, and FOR treats that as the point — appetite is the suspect, not the guide (Movement 2). AGAINST names the cost: no term for rest, refusal, or legitimate want, and no audit of duties imposed by those who benefit from them. The record shows the poem honoring self-forgetting to the very gate of heaven (Movement 10) and never once pricing what the forgetting costs the forgetter. **Edge: open — the poem's silence here is the loudest thing in the file.**
- **A point of agreement worth recording:** neither side defends the desire-question. "What do I want to do today?" loses on both readings — FOR by the trunk-and-Mercedes pricing (Movement 11), AGAINST by omission. The contest is entirely over what replaces appetite, and whether the replacement can be trusted.
- **What remains open:** (1) whether a daily form can carry a crisis ethics, or whether the question is honestly a fair-weather instrument; (2) whether the question needs a second daily clause — an audit of the duty-list itself — which the poem seems to supply only in the extreme form of Mandavya's curse; (3) whether fulfillment is its own justification when hearers refuse it, as Vidura's life asks and neither case fully answers; (4) whether the self's absence from the question is the epic's wisdom or its blind spot.
