# Q7 — Who Should You Be Loyal To, and Why?

**DRAFT — Robert Tilford's Cecil/Mahabharata essay campaign, Deep-Dive Group C (the loyalty cluster). Drafted 2026-10-21; NOT published. The question is Wes Cecil's; the FOR and AGAINST cases were built independently from the lecture record, and the CROSS is the coordinator's comparison. Cecil quoted only in short phrases, with timestamps or movement numbers. Interpretation is marked "(interpretation)" where it occurs.**

**The question (Cecil's words, lecture "The Ancients: Vyasa and The Mahabharata," ~42:10, cluster 42:10–42:27):** "Who should you be loyal to, and why?"

---

## FOR

**Thesis:** Be loyal, the lecture and the epic answer, to the people who make a life — your brothers and companions, the friend who stood by you, and the guide who gives you wisdom — because in this poem people are not an addition to wealth, power, and paradise; they are those things themselves (Movements 8–11). Exile is bearable together and grief-stricken with one man missing (Movement 9); heaven refused alone is the final test passed (Movement 10, Svargarohana road); and a king who dishonours his guide loses his power after the guide leaves (Movements 12–13). Loyalty is therefore not sentiment set against duty. It is duty, rightly understood (Movement 7).

### 1. People are the wealth that loyalty protects

Cecil opens the theme as "wealth is people" (Movement 8) and demonstrates it by subtraction in Movement 9. The five Pandava brothers and their wife Draupadi endure twelve years of exile in the wilderness, and the record of their lament is precise: they do not mind being exiled when they are all together, because they have Draupadi and they have their brothers. When Arjuna leaves for the Himalayas to seek weapons — the austerities, and the Kirata fight, at the width Movement 9 gives them — the others fall into grief. It is not being exiled that is bad, the movement teaches; it is not having those we love around us. (interpretation) If joy can leave a company that has already lost kingdom and home, simply because one person leaves, then that person was the wealth all along — and loyalty to him is not decoration on a good life, it is custody of the good itself.

### 2. Heaven alone is no heaven

The poem saves its strongest proof for its last test (Movement 10, the Svargarohana road). The brothers fall on the road to paradise; Yudhishthira, the eldest and most noble, makes it. Shown his brothers suffering in the hell-vision, he refuses the offer to return to heaven without them: he would rather suffer in hell with those he loves than be in heaven on his own, because — in Cecil's phrase — "being in heaven alone is no heaven" (Movement 10). The record's margin note adds the first of the two refusals at heaven's gate: the stray dog who followed him, whom Yudhishthira refuses to abandon as Indra's price of entry, and who is Dharma in disguise. The man who will not leave a dog will not leave his brothers. (interpretation) Loyalty here is measured at the highest possible price — paradise itself — and the poem counts paying it as passing, not failing.

### 3. Refuse to be split for advantage

Cecil poses the loyalty question directly in the cluster at 42:10–42:27, and in the discussion passage at 44:34 he tells of six brothers/friends facing a college choice who refuse to be divided by it: "we will be going together" (44:34). As the story is relayed in the evidence, the offer made to some is answered for all — if you reject even one of us, we will not come. (interpretation) This is Movement 9 and Movement 10 translated into an ordinary decision: an opportunity that requires abandoning one of your company is not an opportunity but a subtraction, and the loyal answer treats the company as indivisible because the company is the good being pursued.

### 4. Be loyal to the guide, because power follows company

Movement 11 states the positive side: the people to prize are those "who give you wisdom" and grant you insight, held "as wealth," as true wealth. Movements 12–13 show what disloyalty to such a person costs. Indra, king of the gods, receives his guru Brihaspati and does not rise to show him respect; the sage reads the slight, leaves quietly, and Indra's power begins to fade (Movement 13). Movement 12 draws the chain explicitly: wealth threatens judgment; failed judgment insults or forgets the people around you; they leave; and then all your power goes away. Movement 14 completes the lesson — Indra, mistrusting his replacement guru Vishvarupa, kills him, and Cecil's catalogue of the undoing is "arrogance. Fear. Envy." (interpretation) Power in this poem is downstream of company, always in that order: keep the wise person in the room, honoured, and power stays; slight him, and no title holds it.

### 5. Karna: loyalty as the debt honour pays

The poem's standing case is Karna — the "kind of six" brother of Movement 6, the brother killed unknowing in Movement 7, and the subject of the story-voiced question at 41:15. Duryodhana gave Karna a kingdom when the world mocked his birth. Karna's loyalty to that friend is chosen and held at the highest cost. (interpretation) At its strength, this is loyalty as repayment in kind: the one who recognised you when recognition was refused everywhere else has a claim on you that later advantage cannot outbid, because what he gave was not a thing but standing itself. To abandon such a benefactor for a better offer would repeat, in reverse, the world's original act — valuing the man by his price rather than by his bond.

### 6. Loyalty is dharma, particular and owed

None of this is vague goodwill. Movement 5 teaches that dharma is particular: your duty is built around who you are and who you are interacting with, so each person stands in different obligations to different people. Movement 7 gives Krishna's answer to Arjuna — duty rightly understood binds even when it is hardest. (interpretation) Read together, the movements answer Cecil's question at 42:10 with a method as well as a list: you should be loyal to your own — the companions whose absence empties exile (Movement 9), the brothers whose absence empties heaven (Movement 10), the benefactor who gave you standing (Karna, 41:15), and the guide whose presence sustains power (Movements 13–14) — because the epic locates the good life, and even paradise, inside those bonds, and nowhere else.

---

## AGAINST

**Thesis.** The strongest case against loyalty is not that people feel it too little, but that the *Mahabharata*, as Wes Cecil presents it, shows loyalty working too well — binding a person to a preferred few, to an assigned station, and to a wrongful benefactor, until the bond itself overrides judgment. On the evidence of the lecture, loyalty is not a free-standing virtue. It is partial by definition, it can be conscripted by duty (Movement 7), it is allotted rather than chosen (Movement 5), and when held perfectly — in Karna — it helps produce catastrophe.

### 1. The poem punishes partial loyalty on the final road

Movement 10's margin notes record the falls on the road to paradise, each with an assigned cause in the text. Draupadi falls *first*, and her cause is partiality toward Arjuna. The sequence matters. The company does not lose its most disloyal member first; it loses the figure whose failing was preferential love — loving one brother above the others. If loyalty's natural form is preference for one's own, the poem treats that preference not as innocence but as a defect serious enough to end the journey (Movement 10, margin note ii).

### 2. Duty does not protect loyalty to persons — it orders their killing

In Movement 7, Krishna's answer to Arjuna on the field is, in Cecil's summary, "you need to kill your family." Arjuna's loyalty to brothers, uncles, and teachers is explicitly subordinated to warrior duty in a just war. Cecil frames the central question at ~42:10 — "Who should you be loyal to, and why?" — but Movement 7 has already exposed the trap inside it: a higher claim can always be invoked to dissolve loyalty to actual persons, while demanding a harder loyalty to role and cause in their place. The person is expendable; the abstraction is not.

### 3. Loyalty's objects are assigned, not chosen

Movement 5 states that dharma is particularized by caste, sex, age, and station — duty and obligation are "built around who you are." A loyalty whose object is assigned before reflection begins is not a relationship a person has examined and affirmed (interpretation). It is an inheritance enforced as identity. That is why the exit question is decisive (interpretation): a chosen bond can be re-chosen; an assigned bond that forbids leaving has become a cage, and calling the cage loyalty only decorates it.

### 4. Attachment is named among the interior interferences

Movement 6, developing Movement 5's list, places the obstacles to freedom *inside*: anger, lust, greed, envy, fear, laziness — and, in Arjuna's case on the field, grief and attachment at kingdom scale. Cecil's mirror is exact: the Enlightenment throws off exterior bondage; this tradition throws off interior bondage. Loyalty, when it hardens into attachment, belongs on the interference side of that ledger. It does not simply accompany the failure to do one's duty; in the lecture's own architecture, it *is* one of the things to be overcome (Movements 5–6).

### 5. Karna: loyalty held perfectly, serving adharma

Karna is the counter-case's strongest witness because his loyalty is not base. Duryodhana gave him a kingdom when others mocked his birth; his gratitude is real. He learns the Pandavas are his brothers — his question to his mother stands in the census at 41:15 — and he stays anyway. The result is not that a bad man betrays a good cause. It is that a gifted, grateful man keeps faith perfectly with the wrong patron, fights for adharma, and is destroyed with millions around him. Loyalty did not correct his judgment; it replaced it.

### 6. Cecil himself stages the modern counter

Cecil voices the Enlightenment objection in his own lecture at 35:43–35:58 — "what's more oppressive than other people," "family has to be the most oppressive institution ever created" — labeled here as his rhetorical voicing of that objection, his staged counter-testimony. Its force should not be softened. The family is the institution one does not elect, cannot easily leave, and is taught to obey before one can judge it. Loyalty is the moral name that institution gives its own permanence (interpretation).

### 7. Loyalty is the virtue most easily captured

(Interpretation) Loyalty is uniquely exploitable precisely because it feels like virtue while suspending the question virtue should ask: *is this right?* As moral hazard, it excuses wrongdoing by persons we are bound to — the wrong is re-described as standing by one's own. As capture, it is the first quality institutions and strongmen demand, because a loyal follower need not be persuaded case by case. Karna (41:15) is the epic form; the modern form differs only in scale. A virtue that cannot say no to its object has stopped being a virtue and become a mechanism of obedience.

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **The same exhibits carry both cases.** Karna and Movement 10 are each side's chief evidence. FOR reads Karna as loyalty's noblest form — the debt honour pays (41:15); AGAINST reads the identical loyalty as the catastrophe's instrument — faith kept perfectly with the wrong patron. The disagreement is not over facts but over where the value sits: in the bond itself (FOR) or in the worthiness of its object (AGAINST).
- **Where FOR is strongest:** the poem's own pricing. The final test (Movement 10) measures loyalty against paradise itself and counts the refusal to abandon as *passing*; Movement 9's subtraction is as close as a poem comes to demonstration — remove one person from a company that has lost everything else, and joy, not comfort, is what leaves.
- **Where AGAINST is strongest:** the falls on that same road. Draupadi falls *first*, for partiality — loyalty's natural form, preference for one's own, is the first defect the poem charges (Movement 10, margins). FOR must explain why the poem blesses Yudhishthira's refusal and punishes Draupadi's preference when both are loyalty to persons; its best available answer — his loyalty is equal, hers unequal — is an interpretation, not a text the record carries.
- **The Movement 7 problem stands between them.** Krishna commands killing kin for duty. FOR absorbs this by defining loyalty *as* duty rightly understood — but that move costs FOR its plainest evidence, since the persons loyal feeling would protect are exactly the persons duty orders killed. AGAINST uses the same movement to show loyalty to persons is always revocable by a higher claim. Neither reading removes the other's text.
- **Chosen vs. assigned is the cleanest open seam.** Movement 5 allots duty by caste, sex, age, station; the six at 44:34 *choose* their indivisibility. The lecture holds a birth-assigned loyalty system and a freely chosen loyalty story side by side and never reconciles them. Whether Cecil's question ("who *should* you be loyal to") even has a choosing subject inside the poem's own framework remains open.
- **The exit question is unanswered by both cases.** The only figure in the cluster who leaves is Brihaspati (Movement 13), and he leaves over disrespect shown *to* him — the poem prices the guide's exit, not the follower's. Neither FOR nor AGAINST found evidence for when loyalty itself should end; the poem's silence here may be the question's real remainder.
- **What remains open:** (i) whether duty is loyalty's rival or its highest form; (ii) whether loyalty's value is intrinsic to the bond or wholly borrowed from its object; (iii) whether the 42:10 question is one Cecil expects answered or one he expects carried. The record supports the carrying: the lecture poses the cluster at 42:10–42:27 and answers it with stories on both sides of the line.
