# Q13 — The impaled yogi to the king, and then to Dharma himself

> **DRAFT — Robert's study, Deep-Dive Group E ("the story asking back"), 2026-10-21. Nothing published. Drafted under the Cecil Rule: nothing implied, nothing inferred, everything in evidence; interpretation marked where it occurs.**

**The question** (essay sheet Q13; Wes Cecil, "The Ancients: Vyasa and The Mahabharata," 13:05–14:32): Mandavya, the meditating sage impaled on a king's order for a theft he did not commit, asks the king's men why they ran him through — and then carries the question to the god Dharma himself: why did you run me through? What is wrong with you? How is this just?

**Evidence base:** CECIL-MAHABHARATA-RECORD.md, served excerpts 3 (the impaled yogi), 4 (the apology and the curse on Dharma), 5 (the counselor nobody hears), with the desk's identification and fork notes.

**The fork, carried unmerged throughout:** in the epic's own text (Adi Parva) Mandavya's curse lands on **Vidura** — Dharma born of a servant mother as the curse's measure. **Yudhishthira** is a different strand: Dharma's *son* by Kunti. Cecil's telling fuses the two. The FOR case below rests on the epic's Vidura strand; the fusion is named wherever it would otherwise do silent work.

---

## FOR — the poem answers Mandavya

The question is posed in the record in two places. In the served excerpt 3, the impaled yogi, a meditating sage is impaled on a king's order after thieves hide stolen gold in his temple. In the served excerpt 4, the apology and the curse on Dharma, he absolves the king, who made an honest mistake, and goes to Dharma himself. The story-voiced questions stand in the lecture's census at 12:44–14:32, and in this question at 13:05–14:32. Mandavya asks why a life spent in meditation and purity was repaid with a spear, and how that repayment is just.

The poem gives an accounting first. In excerpt 4, Dharma answers that the suffering repaid a childhood act: Mandavya as a child killed insects. Cecil's telling adds "and a bird"; the record's desk note states that the standard account's childhood cruelty is to insects, pierced with a reed or blade of grass. The answer is therefore not silence and it is not mystery. Dharma names a cause, a prior act, and a repayment imposed for that act. The ledger, as Dharma presents it, balances.

Mandavya then judges the ledger itself. In excerpt 4 he rejects the proportion outright, names Dharma's error as an error of thinking — "you have lost your way, god of dharma" (13:05–14:32) — and, in the fuller epic telling recorded in the desk margin to excerpt 3, he lays down a limit on karmic liability for the young. That limit is part of the answer. The poem does not simply repeat Dharma's accounting and close the case. It records a mortal sage amending the rule under which he was punished, because the punishment exceeded the offence committed by a child.

The curse carries the answer forward into a life. In excerpt 4 Mandavya curses Dharma to be reborn in the wheel of life, so that he can learn again and understand better. THE FORK must be stated here. In Cecil's telling that rebirth becomes Yudhishthira. In the epic's own text, Adi Parva as recorded in the desk fork note to excerpt 4, the curse lands on Vidura: Dharma is born as Vidura, of a servant mother, as the curse's measure — "born in a servant's womb" — fits its cause. Yudhishthira is a different strand, Dharma's son by Kunti. The FOR case rests on the epic's Vidura strand.

Read so: the working-out is Vidura's whole career in the poem, given in the served excerpt 5, the counselor nobody hears. Vidura is consulted by all, warns against the gambling, warns against Duryodhana's course, and is ignored over and over. He is the speaker of the *Vidura Niti* in the Udyoga Parva. Excerpt 5 states the principle his life demonstrates: "even the god of Dharma is subject to dharma." Interpretation: the god who once imposed a disproportionate repayment from above it now lives under dharma from below it — low-born by the curse's measure, right in counsel, and without power to make his counsel prevail. He learns the limit not as a theory but as a mortal condition.

That is the poem's oblique answer across the whole epic. It does not acquit Mandavya by denying karma, and it does not vindicate Dharma by repeating his accounting. It lets the accounting stand, lets Mandavya overrule its proportion, changes the law for the young, and then makes Dharma himself live inside the amended order as Vidura.

**Three strongest evidence anchors:**

- Excerpt 4 (13:05–14:32): Dharma gives an accounting — childhood killing of insects — and Mandavya rejects its proportion.
- Desk margin to excerpt 3 and fork note to excerpt 4, Adi Parva: Mandavya lays down a limit on karmic liability for the young and curses Dharma to mortal birth as Vidura, not Yudhishthira.
- Excerpt 5: Vidura, Dharma reborn low, counsels against the dice game and the war, is heard by all and obeyed by none — dharma subject to dharma, enacted.

## AGAINST — the question stands open

Mandavya's question is asked twice in the served record, and it is not answered either time. In excerpt 3, the impaled yogi, he wakes from meditation on the spear and asks, mildly, why they ran him through. In excerpt 4, the apology and the curse on Dharma, he carries the same question higher: he has done everything right, meditating and pure, and he gets run through with a spear — what gives? (13:05–14:32). The king cannot answer it. Dharma's reply does not answer it either. It only names a cause.

That cause is the childhood-insects ledger. Dharma tells him that as a child he killed insects — Cecil's telling adds a bird — and that this suffering is the imposed repayment (excerpt 4). Interpretation: a ledger entry is not a moral justification. It states that the account balances inside the framework; it does not show that impalement is a proportionate, let alone a just, return for a child's cruelty to insects. The disproportion is the whole of the episode, and naming the earlier act does not shrink it. The poem's own figure says so inside the story: Mandavya rejects the proportion outright and calls Dharma's position an error of thinking — "you have lost your way, god of dharma" (13:05–14:32). The answer is refused by the man who received it, on the record, in the scene itself.

Notice where the apology in this episode actually occurs, and where it does not. In excerpts 3 and 4 the king runs to the temple and apologizes profusely, and Mandavya absolves him entirely: honest mistakes happen; sometimes you kill the innocent. The human who ordered the impalement concedes his error. Dharma, in the served record, concedes nothing. He offers the repayment claim and nothing after it — no admission that the proportion was wrong, no apology, and no restitution to Mandavya. Nothing in excerpts 3 or 4 records the suffering being undone, compensated, or even acknowledged as excessive by the god who imposed it.

Notice also who does the repair work. It is not Dharma. It is the victim. Mandavya curses Dharma to be reborn in the wheel of life so that he can learn again and understand better (excerpt 4). In the epic's fuller telling, as the desk margin to excerpt 3 records, the same act lays down a limit on karmic liability for the young. Interpretation: the framework is amended by the man it wounded, acting in anger from the spear's aftermath — not corrected from above by the god who administered it. And the amendment faces forward. A limit on liability for future children protects children who come after. It does not repay Mandavya. His impalement stands in his own life unredeemed, and the rule that would have spared him exists only because he suffered under the rule that did not.

THE FORK, unmerged: in the epic's own text (Adi Parva) the curse lands on Vidura; Cecil's telling fuses the rebirth into Yudhishthira. On the epic's line, excerpt 5 shows what the rebirth produces: Vidura, the counselor everybody consults and nobody hears, warning against the gambling and the war, ignored over and over. Argument, not evidence: even if Cecil's fusion were adopted, it would not close the question — a god reborn to learn does not retroactively justify what was done to the teacher who had to curse him into learning it.

Read so: the poem gives Mandavya a cause, a curse, and a forward-looking amendment. It does not give him a justification he accepts, a concession from Dharma, or restitution for himself. The question stands open.

**Three strongest evidence anchors:**

- Excerpt 4 (13:05–14:32): Dharma's whole answer is the childhood-insects ledger, and Mandavya rejects its proportion outright in the scene.
- Excerpts 3–4: the king apologizes and is absolved; Dharma concedes no error, offers no apology, and no restitution to Mandavya is recorded.
- Excerpt 3 margin and excerpt 5, with the fork kept open: the repair — a limit on karmic liability for the young, and in the epic the curse landing on Vidura, the unheard counselor — is Mandavya's work, protects others later, and leaves his own case unanswered.

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **FOR is strongest on sequence.** No other episode in the record makes the framework *respond* to a mortal's objection: an accounting is given (excerpt 4), judged disproportionate by its recipient, amended for the young (excerpt 3 margin), and the god is made to live under the amended order as Vidura (excerpt 5). The answer is structural, spread across the epic, and the record documents every link.
- **FOR's strongest single fact:** excerpt 5's own principle — "even the god of Dharma is subject to dharma" — is not a desk inference; the lecture states it, and Vidura's career enacts it. The FOR case is at full strength when it rests there and on the fork kept straight (Vidura, not Yudhishthira).
- **AGAINST is strongest at the scene's center.** The answer is refused by the man who received it, in the scene itself (excerpt 4). Whatever the framework later does, at the moment of asking, the questioner says the proportion is an error — and the record contains no concession, apology, or restitution from Dharma to set against that refusal.
- **AGAINST's strongest single fact:** the repair is the victim's work. The limit on liability for the young and the curse that educates the god are both Mandavya's acts, done from the spear's aftermath; and the amendment faces forward — it protects children who come after and does not reopen Mandavya's own case.
- **Where the two actually divide:** on what counts as an answer. FOR counts outcomes — the law changed, the god educated, the disproportion never repeatable in the same form. AGAINST counts the scene — a justification the sufferer can accept, offered by the party who imposed the suffering. The record supplies FOR's kind of answer in full and AGAINST's kind not at all. Interpretation: each side is undefeated on its own measure, and the poem declines to supply the shared measure that would settle it.
- **What remains open:**
  - Whether Vidura's career reads as Dharma's education (FOR) or as a second disproportion — the god reborn right, low, and powerless to prevent the war he warns against (AGAINST). Excerpt 5 supports both readings; the record does not choose.
  - Cecil's fusion (Yudhishthira) would make the FOR case look simpler, which is exactly why the desk keeps the fork unmerged: the simpler answer is the lecture's compression, not the epic's text.
  - Mandavya's own restitution. Nothing in the record offers any — to either side. Printed as open, under the Cecil Rule.
