# Q9 — Who Do You Take With You? Who Is Important to You?

**DRAFT — Robert Tilford's Cecil/Mahabharata essay campaign, Deep-Dive Group C (the loyalty cluster). Drafted 2026-10-21; NOT published. The question is Wes Cecil's; the FOR and AGAINST cases were built independently from the lecture record, and the CROSS is the coordinator's comparison. Cecil quoted only in short phrases, with timestamps or movement numbers. Interpretation is marked "(interpretation)" where it occurs.**

**The question (Cecil's words, lecture "The Ancients: Vyasa and The Mahabharata," ~42:18, pair at ~42:27):** "Who do you take with you? Who's important to you?" — with its second half in the same breath: "who do you defend and trust and support; who defends and trusts and supports you."

---

## FOR

**Thesis:** The lecture's answer does not identify the important people by rank, usefulness, or even closeness of blood; it identifies them by a refusal. The important ones are those you will not advance without — whose absence turns exile into grief and heaven into no heaven, and whom you will not trade away for a prize, up to and including paradise itself (interpretation). Cecil poses the question at 42:18 — "Who do you take with you? Who's important to you?" — and gives its measure in the same breath at 42:27: "who do you defend and trust and support; who defends and trusts and supports you." The epic then proves the definition at the extremes: on the forest road, at heaven's gate, and in hell.

### 1. Absence, not exile, is the grief — Movement 9

In Movement 9, the five Pandava brothers and Draupadi are exiled twelve years in the wilderness, and Arjuna leaves for the Himalayas to win weapons. The company does not grieve the exile itself: "we don't mind being exiled here in the wilderness when we're all together." What breaks them is the subtraction of one member — "now that Arjuna's gone, the joy has gone out of our lives." The record's margin note names the method exactly: the theme is "demonstrated by subtraction," by removing one person from a company that has already lost everything else. Importance, in this movement, is measured by what an absence does to a place that circumstances alone could not ruin (interpretation).

### 2. Heaven itself is re-priced — Movement 10

Movement 10, the final test, carries the test to its highest price. On the last road the brothers fall one by one; Yudhishthira alone reaches heaven, is shown his brothers suffering in hell, and refuses the exchange offered him: "I would rather suffer in hell with those I love and care for than be in heaven on my own." Cecil's verdict on the scene: "It is only a paradise if you're with your brothers." In the epic's own mechanism, as the record notes, the hell is a vision-test arranged by Dharma, and Yudhishthira's refusal to abandon them dissolves it — the brothers are found in heaven after all. The refusal is not rewarded incidentally; the refusal *is* the passing of the test (interpretation).

### 3. The circle extends past kin, to the stranger at the gate — Movement 10

The same movement contains the first of the poem's two refusals, supplied in the record's margin note: the stray dog who follows Yudhishthira on the road, and whom he refuses, at heaven's very gate, to abandon as the price of entry set by Indra. The dog is Dharma in disguise. The record states the doubling plainly: "the man who will not leave a dog will not leave his brothers; the poem tests the same refusal twice, at increasing weight." Who you take with you is therefore not bounded by family, merit, or species of claim — it reaches the unregarded companion who simply walked beside you (interpretation).

### 4. The doctrine is stated outright: people are the wealth — Movements 8 and 11

What the episodes demonstrate, the lecture also states. Movement 8 opens the theme — "wealth is people" — under the chapter heading "People as true wealth" (24:48), and Movement 11 states it against the money-world: being with those you care about, "who give you wisdom, who grant you insight," is wealth "as true wealth, as the foundation of the quality of your life." The modern inversion Cecil names — pursue money and things, and expect the people to follow — is, on the lecture's showing, exactly backwards.

### 5. The one you fail to honour is the one whose leaving ruins you — Movements 12–13

Movements 12–13 give the doctrine its causal chain. Movement 12: wealth threatens judgment; threatened judgment insults, mistreats, or forgets the people around you; "and then they leave, and then all your power goes away." Movement 13 shows the chain in heaven itself: Indra, king of the gods, does not rise to show respect to his guru Brihaspati, who reads the slight and simply leaves — no argument, no fight. The record's margin note fixes the order: "lose the person, lose the power — in that order, always in that order." To fail to take someone with you by respect is already to have lost them, and everything that rested on them (interpretation).

### 6. Trust is owed even to the outsider taken in — Movement 14, and the census at 44:34

Movement 14 completes the affirmative on trust, the question's second half (42:27). Indra's replacement guru is Vishvarupa, of asura descent on his mother's side, taken in from the enemy's kin; he advises well and order returns, but Indra "never quite trusts him," suspects him, and kills him — Cecil's verdict: "Oh, big mistake." The record notes the epic gives the suspicion a hook, yet the killing remains the catastrophe, the gravest sin in the poem's ledger, and it engenders Vritra, a worse enemy than the one imagined. Trust, once a person is taken in, is not conditional on their origin (interpretation). The census point at 44:34 seals the case in advance of all these episodes: "we will be going together" — taking-with-you as a refusal, stated before any test arrives, to arrive separately at all.

## AGAINST

**Thesis:** Taken as a literal doctrine, Cecil's question at ~42:18 — "Who do you take with you? Who's important to you?" (pair at 42:27) — fails on the poem's own evidence. In the Mahabharata as Cecil tells it, no one succeeds in carrying his company through: the road strips the travellers one by one, the war leaves millions behind, the dearest brother is the one never taken at all, and even heaven's final scene does not show a group rescued by loyalty — it shows one man tested by an illusion (interpretation). What the poem honors is a refusal. What it never grants is the carrying.

### 1. The last road leaves them one by one

Movement 10, the final test, is the counter-case in narrative form. On the road to paradise the company is *not* all taken. The record's margin notes name the falls and their assigned causes in the text: Draupadi falls (partiality toward Arjuna), Sahadeva falls (pride in his wisdom), Nakula falls (pride in his beauty), Arjuna falls (the unkept boast to burn his enemies in a day), Bhima falls (gluttony and pride). Yudhishthira arrives essentially alone, with a stray dog. If "take with you" were achievable, this is where the poem would achieve it. Instead each companion is left where a specific flaw drops him or her — the poem assigns the leavings reasons, and none of the reasons is that Yudhishthira chose badly. They fall despite the most loyal brother in the epic walking beside them.

### 2. The "taking" is a test, not a rescue

Movement 10, margin note (i), gives the mechanism: in the Svargarohana Parva the hell is a *vision-test*, arranged by Dharma himself. Yudhishthira hears his brothers' and Draupadi's voices in torment, refuses heaven rather than abandon them — and the illusion dissolves; the brothers are found in heaven after all. So no one was actually carried out of hell by his refusal. They were never there. What the scene proves is the quality of one man's refusal, not the efficacy of taking: the poem's own mechanics deny that the group was rescued by him. A doctrine built on this scene must admit that its climax is a test of intention staged over people who did not, in the event, need taking (interpretation).

### 3. The war is the mass refutation

Movement 7, Krishna's answer on the field, supplies the numbers the question must survive — and does not. Cecil's count, hedged twice and corrected by the desk check: the hundred sons of Gandhari are all killed; almost the entire Pandava army is destroyed; countless uncles are killed. The Kurukshetra field is the place where "who do you take with you?" receives its bloodiest negative: millions are *not* taken with anyone. They are left on the field by both sides, in a war the poem calls just and a duty. If universal taking were the poem's law, its central event would be its central crime. The poem instead lets duty command the leaving — and the killing.

### 4. Arjuna's people are the people he must kill

Movement 6 sets the crisis that the Gita answers: Arjuna lowers his bow because those he would take with him — half-brothers, uncles, kin he grew up with — stand on the other side, to be killed *by him*. Movement 7 gives Krishna's answer: do your warrior's duty anyway. The structure is fatal to any simple reading of the question. The poem does not resolve Arjuna's grief by letting him take his kin with him; it resolves it by requiring him to leave them in the only way war leaves people. Love of company is named, honored in the grief — and overruled.

### 5. Karna: the voice of the one not taken

At 41:15, in the question census, Karna speaks the poem's most damning line on this question: "where the hell have you been all these years; you sort of left me out in the cold" (Cecil's telling, timestamped in the census). Movement 7's desk check completes it: the brother the Pandavas kill *unknowing* is Karna — Cecil's "kind of six" from Movement 6 — Kunti's firstborn, the hidden eldest. The one person a "take with you" doctrine most needed to reach was never reached, was fought on the other side, and was recognized only after he was beyond taking. Karna is not an exception to the poem's pattern. He is its longest-running instance.

### 6. The chain runs backward: people leave the unworthy

Movements 12–14 reverse the question's direction. In Movement 13, Indra, at his height, does not rise for his guru Brihaspati — and Brihaspati simply leaves. No war, no curse; a quiet exit, and Indra's power drains after the wisdom leaves the room. "Who's important to you?" has an answer *others give about you* (interpretation): importance, in the poem, is forfeited by arrogance. You do not only choose whom to take. You are yourself taken or left, on your conduct — and the poem's king of the gods is left.

### 7. Taken in — and killed by the taker

Movement 14 completes the reversal. The priestless gods take in a replacement guru, Vishvarupa — and Indra, never trusting the half-demon adviser, kills him. Here a man *is* taken with the company, sheltered by it, made its guide — and destroyed by his taker out of suspicion and fear. Being taken, in the poem, is no guarantee of safety; being the taker is no guarantee of fidelity. The sin is brahmahatya, priest-killing, and its price is Vritra. The doctrine that staying together is itself the good cannot survive a story in which togetherness is the scene of the murder.

### 8. The modern objection

(Interpretation) Universalized, "take everyone with you" becomes a refusal of all departure: no vocation, no migration, no growth that separates, could ever be innocent. But every life chooses, and Cecil's own framing of modern life concedes it — the college at 43:44: someone decides where you go, and therefore who you leave behind. Selection is not betrayal (interpretation). A doctrine that prices all leaving as failure makes prisoners of the loyal and saints only of those who stay — and the poem, which honors Arjuna's departure to the Himalayas in Movement 9 and commands the war's leavings in Movement 7, never pays that price itself.

## CROSS — the two cases compared

- **The whole dispute turns on one mechanism, read two ways.** Both cases rest on Movement 10, and both accept its margin-noted mechanics: the hell is a vision-test arranged by Dharma, dissolved by the refusal. FOR: the refusal *is* the passing — the scene evidences a disposition, and a disposition is exactly what "who do you take with you" asks about. AGAINST: precisely because it is a test, no taking occurred — the brothers were never in hell, nobody was carried, and a doctrine of carrying cannot be built on a scene where carrying was unnecessary. Same text, opposite conclusions about what the scene is evidence *of*.
- **Where FOR is strongest:** the gates. The dog at heaven's gate (Movement 10) and the hell-refusal are the poem's climax, placed last and weighted most; and Movements 12–13 make "importance" operational rather than sentimental — the important are identifiable in advance by a causal test: slight them and they leave, and power follows them out.
- **Where AGAINST is strongest:** the road and the field. The falls are assigned to the fallen's own flaws — no doctrine of taking survives a poem in which the most loyal brother in the epic arrives alone; and Movement 7's numbers (all hundred sons, nearly the whole army) make the war the question's mass counterexample. FOR's answer — that the question asks about disposition, not outcome — is FOR's strongest and necessary move, and it is marked interpretation wherever it appears.
- **Karna is the question's permanent shadow.** AGAINST fields him at 41:15 — "you sort of left me out in the cold" — the voice of the one not taken. FOR's Group C case does not answer Karna directly; its nearest approach is Movement 14 (trust owed the outsider taken in) and the dog (the circle reaching the claimless). Whether Karna falls under "taken in too late" or under a kinship secrecy no loyalty could have repaired (Movement 7's desk check: killed *unknowing*) remains genuinely open — the poem may intend his case as the limit the doctrine cannot cross.
- **The reciprocity half (42:27) is under-argued by both.** "Who defends and trusts and supports *you*" — FOR answers it through trust (Movement 14), AGAINST through forfeiture (Movement 13: you are left when arrogant). Neither settles how much of "who's important" is chosen by us and how much is decided about us by our conduct. The poem's chain (Movement 12) suggests the two are one question asked from two ends; the record does not close it.
- **A shared concession:** both cases accept that righteous leaving exists — Arjuna's departure (Movement 9) is honored by the poem and grieved by it in the same movement. Any final doctrine of taking must price even justified absence as loss without pricing it as betrayal; neither worker's brief found the poem stating that distinction, and it is the most practical of the open points.
- **What remains open:** (i) achievement vs. disposition — is "take with you" something done or something proven; (ii) Karna's case as the doctrine's limit; (iii) whether selection can ever be innocent inside the lecture's framework, given 43:44 (someone decides who you leave behind) sits in Cecil's own modern passages without condemnation from him in the record as served.
