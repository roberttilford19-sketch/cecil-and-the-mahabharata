## Served excerpt — the background movement (Robert, 2026-10-21 03:54)

*[Transcript stretch of the lecture as Robert served it — Cecil's words, held for Robert's private study under the lane's standing practice; not copied into any publication.]*

The main core narrative of the Mahabharata is the Kurukshetra War, the central narrative. It's like the Iliad in many ways: a civil war of the Kuru clan at Kurukshetra — a civil war of a family, in fact a set of brothers — that seems to relate, like the Iliad, to a likely historical event that was then turned into an epic poem, probably reaching a stable form around 800 BC, between 800 and 400 BC; precisely the timeline of the Iliad, which reached oral form under the "authorship" of Homer and was passed down orally until written down. Around 400 to 200 BC, the second great civilizing and city-building wave of India influenced the poem and brought much more material in — the rise of Jainism and Buddhism — and this is where the Bhagavad Gita is interpolated into the poem, clearly a later addition. Over the next 800 years more material was added as the civilization spread, until it probably stabilized in something like its modern form around 400 CE. The earliest written manuscripts are from 900 CE, clearly copies of earlier versions going back to around 400. Across that span it kept accreting material — legal history, geography, economics, politics, religious speculation — around the central narrative, until it became 18 volumes: an encyclopedia of the world. His recommendation: read a version with the accreted material taken out, leaving the 600–800 page central narrative; that is what most Indians mean by the Mahabharata, since no one outside scholars reads the whole. And his standing warning: anything you say about the Mahabharata is wrong, because some other part of it contradicts it — it is vast, growing, rich, confusing, baffling; "it includes everything. It is the universe."

## Served excerpt — the apology, and the curse on Dharma (Robert, 2026-10-21 03:59)

The king apologizes profusely ("I'm so sorry, it was a mistake"). The yogi absolves him entirely: no fault of yours — honest mistakes; sometimes you kill the innocent; "but I know whose fault I think it is, and I'm going to go talk to him." And so, in Cecil's telling, Dharma is reborn in the Mahabharata as **Yudhishthira**.

The two traditions set as mirror images: the Enlightenment says throw off *exterior* bondage; the Hindu tradition says throw off *interior* bondage. You free yourself by overcoming your baser instincts; you rise in the wheel of dharma, across incarnations, by becoming increasingly good — and you become good by becoming better at fulfilling your duty unimpeded by fear, lust, laziness, greed, hate, anger, undue violence. Krishna's answer is no, no, no: you need to kill your family. This is the central argument. You are a warrior — warrior caste — in a just war; it is your duty to fight and to kill; if you do not do your duty, that is bad, even if it means killing your own family, whom you love and respect. The central moral question: *should I kill my family?* Arjuna, to his credit, says no — and that turned out to be the wrong answer. Cecil's count, hedged twice ("I think"): of the hundred brothers on the other side, ninety-seven are killed and three survive; with their one sister, a hundred and one of them; the sister survives; countless uncles are killed; almost the entire Pandava army is destroyed; and unbeknownst to them they kill one of their own brothers, whom they did not know was their brother. Duty is what matters: fail in your just duty, rightly understood, and it is bad — even in that most extreme case. That is what makes the Gita so central, though it is a later interpolation. Our modern response would be the opposite: question the duty, throw off the shackles — who imposed this caste system anyway? Krishna instead corrects the metaphysics: you misunderstand the world if you think you are killing anybody. You are killing their physical incarnation, which does not matter; the soul is immortal; the wheel goes around again. In killing them rightly you are almost helping them — freeing them to start another life in which they can do better.
